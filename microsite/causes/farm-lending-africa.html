Evaluation — Sep 2026

Pan-African Farm Lending

FinCo (TechnoServe spin-out) proposes a direct-to-farm AI-native lending entity for smallholder farmers in sub-Saharan Africa. Central claim: $33/DCY (DCY) at 1× capital turn, falling to $6.5/DCY if capital recycles 5× as claimed. 6 deep-research runs completed Sep 22, 2026.

Verdict — 6 research runs complete, Sep 22 2026

Decline as structured. FinCo's corrected $/DCY is $80–200 — 8–12× worse than the $6.5–33 headline. Fund One Acre Fund agroforestry first ($31–47/DCY, 2 RCTs). FinCo warrants at most a $1M milestone-gated option bet.

All three load-bearing assumptions failed validation. VCR 3.0 is a triple-selected upper-tail artifact; realistic portfolio VCR is ~1.8, pushing $/DCY to $105–190 and causing net harm to 25–50% of borrowers below VCR 1.55. Capital does not recycle at 5×: FX erosion runs 15–27% in stress years (~1-in-4), and the 4:1 DFI leverage structure makes philanthropic equity the first-loss absorber; treat as near-grant (50–80% nominal recovery over 10 years). The 4% default target sits at the P25, not the median — Apollo Agriculture's real-world rate is 17.7%.

OAF agroforestry dominates FinCo by 2–6× on corrected CE: $31–47/DCY with two RCTs vs. $80–200/DCY for FinCo. FinCo's core differentiator (off-take deduction) is already operational at Emata (Uganda, 94% repayment, entering Tanzania 2026), and FinCo's target bank (NMB Tanzania) just closed $180M in DFI agricultural lending independently. The credit counterfactual for supply-chain-integrated farmers is ~35–45% (not zero); in Kenya it reaches ~62.5%, producing $88/DCY. Babban Gona already reaches 200,000+ Nigerian farmers with 99% repayment and $200M+ deployed. Optimal $30M/yr allocation: 50% OAF trees / 27% OAF core / 13% systems change / 7% seed supply infrastructure / 3% FinCo option bet.

The lending model

FinCo, being spun out of TechnoServe's Strategic Initiatives Group, proposes to bridge a structural gap in African agricultural finance. The problem is straightforward: sub-Saharan Africa has roughly $3B in annual farm lending against an estimated $80B in medium-term need. Capital exists — from development finance institutions (DFIs) like FMO, BII, and Norfund — but it tends to pile up at commercial banks and agri-business buyers rather than reaching the smallholder farmer who actually needs $400 for improved seed and fertilizer before planting season.

The mechanics: an agri-business (say, a rice mill with 5,000 farmer suppliers) provides a "first-loss" guarantee covering the first ~5% of loan losses. FinCo lends $400 per farmer, the agri-business distributes inputs and deducts repayment from harvest proceeds. The farmer never touches cash — repayment is structural, not behavioral. FinCo uses CASA-derived data on agri-business quality to underwrite the supply chain, not the individual farmer.

The phased strategy starts with a 2026 Tanzania prototype (Phase 0), expands to 5–7 banks across multiple markets (Phase 1, 2027), scales to commercial fee-per-loan revenue (Phase 2, 2029), and eventually transitions to a licensed balance-sheet lender (Phase 3, 2030+). The historical analogue cited is ProCredit Holding, which grew from a 1980s IPC consultancy advising Latin American banks into a licensed banking group with a €7.75B portfolio today.

The $580 gain is then translated into DCYs using Kenya World Bank PIP data. A FinCo farmer household consumes approximately $485/person/year ($2,039/household of 4.2). The $580 income gain represents a 28.5% proportional increase, which yields log₂(1.285) = 0.36 doublings per person per year. Across 4.2 household members, that's 1.52 DCY per loan for the farmer household.

FinCo also counts spillover to rural labourers who earn wages from the additional harvest activity ($162 in wages spread across ~2 labourer households), adding 0.93 DCY in spillover — bringing the total to 2.44 DCY/loan. Philanthropic capital per loan is $80 (20% of the $400 loan, with the other 80% from DFI debt). That gives $33/DCY at one capital turn.

The multiplier from recycling is large: if capital turns 5× (FinCo's central assumption, implying 20% annual erosion from FX and credit losses), the same $80 generates 5 × 2.44 = 12.2 DCY, or $6.5/DCY. This would be among the most cost-effective philanthropic interventions in agriculture. The question is whether it is real.

The VCR 3.0 assumption: most contested number in the model

VCR (Value-Cost Ratio) is the pivot on which the whole model turns. The $580 income gain requires VCR = 3.0. At VCR = 2.0 (the minimum threshold), the gain collapses to $180 (net income = $800 revenue − $400 inputs − $180 labour − $40 cost), and the $/DCY approximately triples to ~$100+.

FinCo sources VCR 3.0 from its own CASA program data, where the average observed VCR was 2.98 across engagements where all "enablers" (market access, extension, input bundling) were in place. This is a plausible number given the enabling conditions — but it is drawn from programs TechnoServe chose to engage with, which presumably had the best underlying conditions. This is selection bias, not a portfolio-wide average.

The Duflo-Kremer challenge
FinCo cites Duflo, Kremer & Robinson (2008) in a footnote — but the paper cuts against their VCR claim. The study found fertiliser in Kenya produced 69.5% returns at very low doses (½ teaspoon per plant) but negative returns at the full Ministry-recommended package. FinCo's model assumes farmers receive the optimal input bundle. The evidence suggests that at scale, many borrowers will be at sub-optimal points on the return curve — which compresses portfolio VCR below 3.0.

The "Bold et al. lemon technologies" problem is analogous: agricultural inputs that perform well in supervised trials consistently underperform when scaled without intensive advisory support. FinCo's Phase 0 will use the same intensive CASA-quality engagement that produced VCR 2.98. Phase 2 at 100,000+ farmers will not.

Capital recycling: the 5× turn assumption

FinCo's most distinctive philanthropic claim is that capital is "recyclable" — not a grant, but a self-replenishing vehicle. A five-month crop loan repays at harvest, and the same dollar can be re-lent next season. In theory, $1M of philanthropic capital could turn 2.4× per year (365 ÷ 150 days), implying 12 turns over five years rather than 5. FinCo uses 5× as conservative.

The erosion mechanism is threefold: defaults eating into principal, currency depreciation converting local-currency repayments back to USD, and operational costs. The 20% annual loss assumption (implied by 5 turns over 5+ years) looks manageable in a normal year. It may not be in African currency reality.

In 2022, the Ghanaian cedi depreciated 55% against the dollar. In 2023, the Kenyan shilling lost 25%; the Nigerian naira lost ~40% after the Tinubu float. If FinCo's Tanzania portfolio is denominated in Tanzanian shillings, an average year erodes capital by 8–12% via FX alone before any credit losses. A tail year (25% depreciation + 10% defaults) erodes 35% in a single season — turning 5 cycles of capital into 3 effective ones and pushing $/DCY above $10.

The DFI leverage assumption (80% of the capital stack from DFI debt) is also aspirational at this stage. DFIs typically take 2–5 years to approve a new counterparty. FinCo's Phase 0 is a 2026 Tanzania prototype. Expecting formal DFI partnership at scale by 2029 (Phase 2) is optimistic but not impossible given TechnoServe's existing DFI relationships.

One Acre Fund's agroforestry program is the honest benchmark. It offers a similar headline CE ($31–47/DCY), is backed by two RCTs, and operates at 2.6M adopters. If it doesn't — if FX erosion and defaults consume capital at grant-equivalent rates — OAF agroforestry is strictly better on current evidence.

Open concerns — research in progress

Six research runs launched September 22, 2026 are investigating the following questions. This section will be updated as syntheses come in.

1. VCR selection bias [run]

Research completed. VCR 3.0 is an upper-tail outcome from a triple-selected sample — TechnoServe chose which agri-businesses to engage, chose which CASA programs to run, and averaged over years with good outcomes. The realistic portfolio VCR at scale is approximately 1.8 (range 1.5–2.2), based on five independent evidence sources including the SSA on-farm meta-analysis (CIMMYT/IFPRI), the One Acre Fund bundled input RCT, and AGRA's 18-year $1B program results. This is critical: the VCR-to-$/DCY relationship is violently non-linear. At VCR 3.0 → $33/DCY. At VCR 1.8 → ~$105–190/DCY. Below VCR 1.55, the loan causes net harm. An estimated 25–50% of borrowers at the population median VCR range are likely to lose money on the loan — a sign risk the model ignores entirely.

2. Default rates at comparable lenders [run]

Research completed. FinCo's 4% default target is achievable in a good year — but it is the P25 of the distribution, not the median. Calibrated median is 5–6% (anchored by Emata's ~5–6% and contract farming's 5–15%). The P90 bad-case is 12–18% (consistent with Apollo Agriculture's reported 17.7% default rate and World Bank post-disaster write-off data). Expected mean: ~6–8% per cycle due to fat right tail from correlated drought events.

More concerning: P(capital impairment — defaults exceeding the 5% first-loss cover — at least once in 5 years) is ~90–95%. The first-loss structure has a critical flaw: the agri-business partner providing the guarantee is stressed simultaneously with borrowers in a bad harvest year. This procyclicality means the guarantee is weakest exactly when it is most needed. A severe event (>10% default) has 55–75% probability over five years.

3. FX and capital preservation

East African currencies depreciate 8–25% annually against the dollar in normal-to-bad years. FinCo plans to lend in local currency (Tanzanian shillings, Kenyan shillings). Without hedging instruments (expensive and often unavailable for sub-$10M positions in frontier markets), the "20% annual erosion" assumption that implies 5× capital turns could be right in a good year but catastrophic in a bad one. We are investigating the hedging landscape and comparable agricultural fund capital preservation track records.

4. Credit counterfactual [run]

Research completed. The defensible counterfactual fraction for FinCo's target population (supply-chain-integrated farmers) is approximately 35–45% blended across markets, not FinCo's assumed 0%. This yields a revised $50–58/DCY — roughly 1.5–1.8x the headline. In Kenya specifically, where Apollo Agriculture (350,000+ farmers), OAF/Tupande (~500,000 farmers), and SACCOs already serve the same supply-chain-integrated population, the counterfactual reaches ~62.5%, producing $88/DCY (2.7x headline). Notably, Babban Gona in Northern Nigeria — the closest existing analogue to FinCo's model — already reaches 200,000+ farmers with 99% repayment and $200M+ deployed, directly reducing FinCo's Nigerian additionality.

5. FX and capital preservation [run]

Research completed. 20%/year capital loss is a realistic central estimate for East Africa, not conservative. FX depreciation alone runs 4–5%/year in normal years but hits 15–27% in stress years (Kenya 2023: 27.6%). Combined with agri-SME NPLs and operating costs, total annual capital loss is 13–18% central, 25–45% in stress years. The 5x capital turns assumption holds in median years but breaks in stress years — roughly 1-in-4 to 1-in-5 years. More critically, the 4:1 DFI leverage model amplifies rather than distributes erosion: philanthropic capital subsidizes senior DFI debt while eroding at 60–90%/year on the equity layer. The recommendation: treat philanthropic capital as near-grant (expect to recover 50–80% of nominal over 10 years), not genuinely recyclable.

6. Competitive landscape [run]

Research completed. FinCo's three claimed differentiators collapse under scrutiny. Off-take deduction repayment — their core structural claim — is already proven at Emata (94% repayment vs. Apollo's 82%), which is entering Tanzania in 2026, directly overlapping FinCo's Phase 0 market. NMB Tanzania (FinCo's likely bank partner) just secured $180M from DFIs (FMO, DEG, Proparco) specifically for agricultural lending — 3x FinCo's entire $60M aspiration — rendering the leverage story circular. The CASA data differentiation is unvalidated: no agri-business has confirmed data-sharing, and its underwriting benefit over satellite + mobile data (Apollo) or cooperative delivery data (Emata) is unproven. Recommendation from this run: decline FinCo as structured; redirect to Emata's Tanzania expansion or a narrow FinCo pilot gated on validating the CASA data claim.
