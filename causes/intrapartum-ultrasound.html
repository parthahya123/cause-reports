Intrapartum Sensing & Low-Cost Ultrasound

Two adjacent causes in maternal and neonatal health. Both are underfunded relative to burden. But the investment thesis is different in each case — and the evidence base varies dramatically. Here's what 10 research runs found.
Jargon guide: DALY = one year of healthy life lost to death or disability. $/DALY = cost per healthy year saved; GiveWell's bar for exceptional is ~$50–100. HIE = hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (brain injury from oxygen deprivation at birth). SBBC = Safer Births Bundle of Care. POCUS = point-of-care ultrasound. CTG = cardiotocography (continuous fetal heart rate monitoring). FHR = fetal heart rate.

The problem: birth asphyxia

Birth asphyxia — oxygen deprivation to the baby during labor — kills or permanently injures roughly 2 million children per year globally. About 1 million die as intrapartum stillbirths or in the first 28 days from hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). Another 1 million survive with cerebral palsy, cognitive impairment, or severe epilepsy. The burden is concentrated almost entirely in low- and middle-income countries.

The core clinical problem is straightforward: fetal distress — reduced oxygen supply causing abnormal fetal heart rate patterns — is detectable during labor if you have monitoring equipment. In high-income countries, electronic fetal monitoring (cardiotocography) is universal and intrapartum-related deaths are rare. In LMIC district hospitals, monitoring is a Pinard stethoscope every 30 minutes at best. A baby can slip into irreversible brain injury between checks.

But detection alone doesn't save babies. The evidence shows consistently that the binding constraint is the decision-to-action chain — does the midwife recognize the abnormal reading, interpret it correctly, escalate to a physician, and trigger emergency delivery before injury becomes irreversible? In settings with CTG but no training, CTG uptake, or emergency obstetric capacity, detection equipment sits idle or creates noise without response.

The solution that works: Safer Births Bundle of Care

Of all the technologies and interventions mapped across 10 research runs, one stands out with the clearest evidence and most compelling CE: the Safer Births Bundle of Care (SBBC), developed by Laerdal Global Health with Norwegian funding and validated in a large NEJM trial.

Key finding — NEJM 2025, N=281,165 mothers, 30 facilities in Tanzania

18% reduction in 24-hour perinatal mortality (aRR 0.82, 95% CI 0.73–0.92, p=0.001)
40% reduction in early newborn deaths
75% reduction in in-hospital maternal deaths (aRR 0.25, 95% CI 0.14–0.46)
Cost: $144/DALY | BCR 36 | $4,669 per life saved

Source: Mduma et al., NEJM 2025; CE from Mduma/Lie et al., PLOS ONE 2025

The SBBC is not a single device — it's a four-pillar bundle that addresses both the detection gap and the action gap together:

  • Continuous FHR monitoring — Moyo fetal monitor (~$198/unit), a traffic-light device designed for LMIC midwives, not specialist interpretation
  • Simulation training — NeoNatalie Live (neonatal resuscitation), MamaNatalie (PPH management), MamaBirthie (delivery simulation); targeting decision speed, not just skill
  • Continuous quality improvement — facility dashboards, death audits, peer learning cycles every 2 months
  • Sustainability systems — government integration, local ownership, mentorship networks

The key insight is that the bundle must be deployed together. Deploying Moyo without the training and QI creates CTG paradox — detection without action. The 281k-mother trial is the only large-scale LMIC RCT of intrapartum sensing that shows mortality outcomes. No other intrapartum technology has this evidence.

The c-section misallocation: a separate opportunity

Global c-section rates are severely misallocated. India's private sector runs at 54% (NFHS-6); Telangana reaches 62% urban. Meanwhile sub-Saharan Africa averages 3.5% population-level — a 15× gap. The WHO recommends 10–15%.

The asymmetry that shapes the investment thesis

ARM A — reducing over-use (private sector): ~8.5M excess c-sections/yr globally; ~$5B/yr in unnecessary surgical costs; ~0.1–0.3 DALYs of harm per case (SSI, TTN, PAS risk). Total: ~360k DALYs/yr. A sensing technology operating here saves money and averts morbidity — but achieves ~$1,000+/DALY on health outcomes alone. It's a financial instrument, not a mortality buy.

ARM B — enabling timely c-section (LMIC public sector): ~1.6M deliveries/yr in SSA need but don't get c-sections; ~500k avoidable HIE cases + stillbirths/yr; ~25 DALYs per case. Total: ~20M DALYs/yr. A sensing technology operating here could reach $80–160/DALY — GiveWell-competitive.

ARM B has ~55× more DALYs per case than ARM A. The commercial market (ARM A) can cross-subsidize the philanthropic opportunity (ARM B).

The market failure is structural: payers are in ARM A (private hospitals that can afford devices), while the DALYs are in ARM B (public facilities that cannot). This creates a natural cross-subsidy model: sell devices at commercial prices in India's private sector, use margin to fund deployment in SSA public hospitals.

Where we land on intrapartum sensing

After 5 research runs — Sep 2026 (all complete)

SBBC is the strongest cost-effective intervention we have found across all our cause evaluations. The full CE model (not just the measured Tanzania result) confirms $56–93/DALY for a Moyo-class AMC — GiveWell-competitive. A $30M AMC would avert ~150,000–250,000 perinatal deaths and ~3,000–6,000 maternal deaths over 10 years across 8 target countries. The absorptive ceiling is ~$30–50M philanthropic, catalyzing ~$120–140M total system cost sustained by governments thereafter.

The problem: absent ultrasound in LMICs

Ultrasound is a cornerstone diagnostic in high-income obstetric and emergency care. In LMICs, ~99 million births per year — 76% of all LMIC births — occur without any antenatal ultrasound. The gap is even starker at the primary care level: only ~1.2% of basic LMIC primary care facilities have a functioning ultrasound.

The conditions that ultrasound can diagnose and triage carry substantial mortality burden:

The implied DALY burden: 30,000–60,000 deaths × ~30 DALYs/death = ~0.9–1.8M DALYs/yr attributable to absent LMIC ultrasound — conditional on downstream capacity to act on findings.

The binding constraint: the First Look lesson

Critical finding — First Look RCT, N=46,838, 5 LMICs (Goldenberg et al., BJOG 2018)

Result: NULL on all mortality endpoints from routine antenatal ultrasound.

The trial deployed trained sonographers with portable ultrasound at antenatal care visits in 5 LMICs. Diagnoses were made. Referrals were recommended. But only ~9% of women with a US-diagnosed condition were actually referred, and only 71% of those attended. Effective completion rate: ~6.4% of the at-risk population. When diagnosis doesn't reliably trigger treatment, detection equipment saves ~0 lives.

Implication: the binding constraint is the system downstream of the scan, not the scan itself. Deploying ultrasound without referral chains and surgical capacity is unlikely to reduce mortality in most LMIC settings.

The workforce arithmetic compounds this. To close the ultrasound access gap in the 8 highest-burden countries (~70M births/yr without ultrasound), assuming ~3,750 scans/operator/year, you need ~18,700 additional trained operators. The current training pipeline produces ~1,000–2,000/yr across all countries. Time to close the gap: 10–19 years — and that ignores emigration of trained staff to HICs.

Equipment cost — once considered the binding constraint — is no longer the primary barrier. Handheld ultrasound (Butterfly IQ, GE Vscan Air, Clarius) now costs $1,500–$3,500. At 3,750 scans/year, device amortization is $0.13/scan. The economic barrier is training and system integration, not hardware.

Where we land on low-cost ultrasound

After 5 research runs — Sep 2026 (all complete)

Ultrasound scale-up is a real problem but not a clean mortality buy in the absence of system strengthening. The CE model puts targeted deployment at $171/DALY (surgical-capable facilities) and population-level deployment at $457/DALY — both clearing WHO's India threshold (~$2,000) but not GiveWell-competitive. The First Look null result is the load-bearing constraint: the $/DALY is only achievable if facilities can act on what they diagnose.

AI task-shiftingAI-guided POCUS (Caption Health/GE) could compress training from 3 months to 8 hours for basic obstetric applications. This addresses the workforce bottleneck but doesn't fix the downstream action gap. A necessary but not sufficient condition for scale.
Integrated intrapartum platformReal product opportunity (FHR + intrapartum US + AI, ~$1,000–1,500 total) but premature investment. Moyo has sold only ~739 units in 11 years despite costing $198. Solve deployment before adding platform complexity. Revisit when SBBC reaches 500+ facilities.
Compared to SBBCSBBC is 2–5× more cost-effective and has the mortality RCT. Ultrasound scale-up is complementary infrastructure — valuable for the system but not competitive as a standalone philanthropic bet. Fund SBBC first; ultrasound scales as the system strengthens.
