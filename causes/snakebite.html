Snakebite

138,000 deaths per year globally. A treatment that works. A delivery system that fails. The question is whether philanthropy can fix the delivery system — and under what conditions Africa eventually clears the <$150/DALY bar.
Bottom line

Fund $27M/yr (steady state) + $8M one-time build in India; absorptive ceiling $40M/yr. District QI reaches $110/DALY, CHC ASV banks + transport $207/DALY, NAPSE $134/DALY — all inside the <$150/DALY threshold or close to it, with the portfolio at ~$150/DALY blended. The three tiers work as a system and must be co-funded: transport without ASV at the destination is useless; ASV without clinical protocols kills through under-dosing.

Start Odisha (Year 1), then Bihar (Year 2), then Jharkhand (Year 3). Bihar has the highest absolute death toll (~4,500/yr) but the weakest implementation substrate. Odisha has disaster-status infrastructure, a live ICMR-RMRC QI program, and dual 108 coverage. Manufacture proof cheaply in Odisha, deploy it in Bihar.

Antivenom quality is a free win. Switching from liquid to lyophilized ASV eliminates approximately 19 of the 25 percentage-point field efficacy penalty — the difference between a CHC that nominally stocks ASV and one that actually has potent product when a patient arrives.

Africa: Nigeria and Ethiopia have plausible paths to <$150/DALY under specific conditions. Those conditions are demanding and not yet met. The India portfolio should be the primary bet now; Africa is a 3–5 year horizon.

Global landscape — who is doing what and what we've learned

The WHO NTD designation and what it changed

Snakebite was relisted as a WHO priority neglected tropical disease in 2017, after being removed from the NTD list in 2009 due to—remarkably—"insufficient evidence." The 2017 relisting, following advocacy by the Global Snakebite Initiative and peer-reviewed burden data (Suraweera 2020 eLife), unlocked WHO resources and a formal NTD Road Map 2021–2030 committing to a 50% reduction in snakebite deaths and disability by 2030. As of 2026, the Road Map's targets are significantly off-track.

The antivenom market collapse — why the status quo failed

The central market failure in snakebite is identical to the AMR problem: antivenom generates insufficient commercial revenue to justify production at the volumes needed for public health impact. The crisis became acute in 2014 when Sanofi Pasteur discontinued Fav-Afrique — the only polyvalent antivenom covering the full range of common African snake species (the "Big Four": Echis saw-scaled vipers, Naja cobras, Bitis arietans puff adder, Dendroaspis mambas). Fav-Afrique had been sold at a loss for years; when Sanofi exited, the African antivenom market lost its most effective product. Existing stocks ran out by 2016, creating a crisis MSF has documented with field data on rising deaths in the gap period.

Several products have partially filled the gap:

  • EchiTAb-Plus-ICP (MicroPharm UK / LSTMH / Instituto Clodomiro Picado): the leading West African product, developed specifically for Echis and Bitis envenomation in Nigeria. Efficacy proven in Nigerian RCTs led by LSTMH. Supply is limited — roughly 20,000–30,000 vials/yr — and distribution relies on NGO logistics rather than functional national supply chains.
  • SAIMR Polyvalent (South African Vaccine Producers): southern/eastern African species coverage; limited West African efficacy because venom composition of Echis species differs substantially between regions.
  • Indian polyvalent ASV (VINS, Bharat Serums, Haffkine, Biological E, CRI Kasauli): covers Indian "Big Four" (Naja naja, Bungarus caeruleus, Daboia russelii, Echis carinatus). Sometimes imported to Africa as a desperation fill — species coverage is wholly inappropriate for African envenomation and this is dangerous.
  • New developer pipeline: Vhyrex/VASG (thermostable, pan-African, Phase 1); VenomAid synthetic small-molecule candidates; recombinant monoclonal antibody (mAb) cocktail platforms at DTU Copenhagen and UCB — all pre-market, earliest plausible arrival 2028–2030.

The Wellcome Trust £80M programme — what it's funding and what it's not

In May 2019, Wellcome announced an £80 million, 7-year investment (2019–2026) in snakebite — approximately 2.5× the total funding the field had received in the prior decade. This is the dominant philanthropic actor in snakebite globally and has shaped the entire research agenda:

  • Ophirex (varespladib): Wellcome awarded Ophirex funding to advance oral and IV varespladib — a broad-spectrum phospholipase A₂ inhibitor — through clinical trials. Varespladib is not an antivenom; it targets a shared enzyme across many venom types, which is why it could theoretically be a universal first-line treatment before species-specific ASV is available. Phase 2 trials ongoing.
  • Recombinant mAb platforms: Grants to DTU (Technical University of Denmark) and UCB for oligoclonal antibody cocktails targeting conserved venom toxin families. The concept: produce highly defined human antibody mixtures that neutralize the 5–10 toxin types responsible for most mortality across species. Still pre-clinical for most venom types; African three-finger toxins are proving harder than expected.
  • LSTMH multi-centre antivenom trial in Africa (2023): A Wellcome-funded trial involving LSTMH, Kenya Snakebite Research and Intervention Centre, KEMRI-Wellcome Programme (Kenya), Bayero University (Nigeria), Kwame Nkrumah University (Ghana), and UCL to generate the first rigorous head-to-head efficacy data comparing available African antivenoms under controlled conditions. Results expected 2026–27.
  • RSTMH small grants 2021–23: 10 small grants to African, Asian, South American, and European researchers across clinical, epidemiological, and social-science snakebite research — building the global researcher pipeline the field chronically lacked.
  • Target Product Profiles (TPP): WHO/Wellcome co-funded a rigorous exercise to define exactly what an ideal snakebite treatment would look like (thermostability, dosing schedule, species coverage, price ceiling) to orient developers. Published 2022; now the reference document for the field.

What Wellcome is NOT doing: delivery. The £80M programme is almost entirely R&D and pipeline-building. It is not funding ASV procurement, supply chain strengthening, or CHC stocking programs. There is an explicit assumption that if better products exist, health systems will adopt them — but in India and Africa, the bottleneck is not product quality, it is the absence of functioning antivenom logistics at the facility level. Wellcome's programme and the India portfolio in this investment case are essentially non-overlapping.

What has been tried and learned — the failures matter

Three decades of snakebite intervention produce both confirmed approaches and clear dead ends. Understanding the dead ends is critical for not repeating them:

  • Community health worker (CHW)-carried ASV — failed at scale. Multiple programs have trained village health workers to carry liquid ASV vials and administer them in the field. These fail consistently for two reasons: (a) liquid ASV requires cold chain that CHWs cannot maintain; (b) ASV administration requires clinical monitoring that untrained CHWs cannot safely provide — anaphylaxis occurs in ~5–10% of patients and requires adrenaline and airway management. CHW-carried lyophilized ASV without IV access and monitoring capability kills through anaphylaxis as often as it saves through early treatment. The causal chain to hospital remains essential.
  • Mobile snake identification apps — not actionable. Several apps were developed to allow victims or bystanders to photograph the snake and receive species identification and treatment guidance. Failed in field conditions: rural snakebite areas typically have poor connectivity; identification requires a clear photo of the snake (which is not obtained in the chaos following a bite); even correct identification doesn't change first-line management in a setting without ASV. None showed mortality impact; some were actively counterproductive by delaying transport while attempting identification.
  • Rubber boots and protective footwear programs — worn but not effective. India distributes rubber boots to agricultural workers in high-snakebite districts (Bihar, Odisha). The problem: rice paddy work and most agricultural labor is done bare- or sandal-footed because rubber boots in 40°C heat are not tolerable for 8–10 hour workdays. Distribution uptake data looks good; actual wear rates in field conditions are low. Not a scalable mortality intervention.
  • Tourniquet de-training — partial success. Traditional first-aid in South Asia includes applying tight tourniquets to envenomed limbs, which causes tissue necrosis and dramatically worsens outcomes (especially for cytotoxic bites). WHO and government programs have invested in tourniquet de-training and are making some progress, but deeply embedded traditional practice is slow to change — even healthcare providers trained in modern protocols sometimes apply tourniquets in panic. This is worth continuing but has a multi-decade time horizon to population-level behavior change.
  • Community awareness without end-to-end system — zero mortality effect. This is the most replicated null finding in the snakebite literature. Multiple programs — across India, Africa, and Southeast Asia — demonstrate that teaching communities to recognize snakebite signs and seek care, without ensuring that the facility they reach has potent, properly stored antivenom and trained staff, produces zero mortality improvement. The demand side is not the binding constraint.
  • Antivenom potency varies enormously and is under-tested. The Toxicon Big-Four QA analysis found significant between-batch protein concentration variation and geographic venom composition mismatches in Indian polyvalent ASV. Programs that assume "stocked ASV = potent ASV" systematically overestimate effective coverage — this is an invisible failure mode.
  • Cold chain failure at CHC level is endemic. Liquid ASV programs repeatedly fail because CHC refrigeration fails in field conditions — summer heatwaves, power outages, and load-shedding all drive temperature excursions. Lyophilized formulation (ambient-stable up to 56°C in accelerated testing) is the only durable CHC-level solution, eliminating this failure mode entirely.
  • Near-zero hospital CFR is achievable — but requires clinical protocols, not just ASV supply. The Demow CHC proof-of-concept (Toxicon 2025) demonstrates zero snakebite deaths over 4 years in a rural Indian CHC through protocol-concordant management. Clinical training matters as much as product supply: adequate dose, appropriate species recognition, neurotoxic monitoring, and FFP for hemotoxic coagulopathy are all skill-based, not supply-based constraints.

The delivery gap — India primary field data

The ICMR National Task Force cross-sectional survey (Nat Commun 2025, 25 districts, 11 states, 60M population, 7,094 cases) defines where the portfolio's three tiers intervene:

  • 86.4% sought hospital care — demand is not the problem
  • Only 60.2% received ASV — a ~26pp gap representing missing ASV, poor clinical recognition, or protocol failure
  • ~50% of deaths outside hospital — late arrival (transport delay), harmful first-aid (tourniquets, incisions that delay departure), or death in transit

The antivenom mismatch gap — patients who seek care but still die

The ICMR 11-state survey (7,000 cases) found that 86% of snakebite victims sought hospital care, but only 60% received antivenom. The obvious interpretation is an access problem — and it is. But there is a second, less-visible gap beneath it: of the 60% who receive antivenom, how many receive the right product, at the right dose, in time? This question was flagged by co-funders as the "lowest-hanging fruit" and the key cost-effectiveness signal — because fixing a mismatch for a patient already in the building is far cheaper than fixing access.

Three layers of mismatch

Layer 1: Species mismatch — product covers wrong snake

Indian polyvalent ASV covers the "Big Four" (Naja naja, Bungarus caeruleus, Daboia russelii, Echis carinatus). In practice, roughly 15–20% of serious envenomations in India involve species outside this panel — including Trimeresurus pit vipers in the Northeast and Ophiophagus (king cobra) in forested areas. These patients receive the standard polyvalent, which may provide no neutralisation for the actual venom. The clinical team usually doesn't know: most Indian facilities lack systematic snake identification. The patient gets the treatment protocol, appears to have received antivenom, and dies anyway — recorded as "antivenom failure."

In Africa the mismatch problem is structurally more severe: Indian polyvalent ASV is sometimes imported as a gap-fill for Nigeria and Ethiopia, despite having zero efficacy against African species. Any patient receiving this product is harmed by the delay to appropriate treatment while appearing to be treated.

Layer 2: Dose mismatch — under-dosing is the dominant clinical error

The most common clinical error in snakebite management is not absence of antivenom but under-dosing. WHO guidelines require dosing to clinical endpoint (20WBCT normalisation for hemotoxic bites; clinical paralysis reversal for neurotoxic bites), which may require 10–30+ vials. In practice, many district hospitals administer a fixed "starter" dose of 8–10 vials and stop — either because they're rationing limited stock, because the clinical team doesn't know to keep going, or because the patient (or family) refuses further treatment after partial improvement.

The Toxicon Big-Four QA analysis and the ICMR clinical audit data both show guideline-concordant dosing at fewer than 50% of district hospitals. Under-dosing in hemotoxic bites leads to ongoing coagulopathy, delayed bleeding, and death hours after discharge — deaths that are recorded as community deaths rather than in-facility antivenom failures and therefore disappear from the numerator of "received antivenom" statistics.

Layer 3: Timing mismatch — antivenom given, but too late to reverse neurotoxic binding

For neurotoxic bites (Bungarus, Naja), ASV is effective only against circulating free venom — once the toxin binds the neuromuscular junction, antivenom cannot reverse it. The therapeutic window is effectively ≤2–4 hours for Bungarus β-neurotoxins. Rural bite-to-facility times in Bihar and Odisha routinely exceed 4–6 hours. Patients who arrive late, receive full-dose antivenom correctly, and are recorded as "received antivenom" may still die from respiratory failure — not because antivenom doesn't work, but because it was given past the point of reversibility.

This timing mismatch is why transport (108) improvement appears in the CEA with a DALY impact disproportionate to its modest cost: it shifts patients from the 6h+ zone into the 1–3h zone where the same antivenom they would have received anyway is actually effective.

Data gap flagged by co-funders
Systematic data on the mismatch layers is sparse. The ICMR survey captures access (who received antivenom) but not appropriateness (species/dose concordance). A targeted clinical audit at 20–30 district hospitals across Odisha and Bihar — recording species identification attempt, vials administered, clinical endpoint reached, and discharge status — would fill this gap and sharpen the cost-effectiveness case for Tier 3 QI. The Granola meeting flagged this as a priority research task. The antivenom mismatch loop (running in parallel to this page) will provide a fuller quantitative estimate when complete.

Anaphylaxis — clarifying the numbers

The meeting referenced a ~40% adverse reaction rate; the relevant distinction is between any adverse reaction and true anaphylaxis. Published clinical trial data on equine polyvalent ASV show:

  • Any adverse reaction (fever, chills, urticaria, rash): 30–60% of patients — these are expected, manageable, and rarely life-threatening with pre-treatment (paracetamol, antihistamine).
  • True early anaphylaxis (hypotension, bronchospasm, angioedema): 5–20% depending on product and population — requires adrenaline and monitoring but is treatable in a supervised clinical setting.
  • Severe systemic anaphylaxis requiring ICU: ~1–3% — the genuinely dangerous tail.

The 40% figure likely refers to the broader adverse-reaction category. This is why CHW-carried antivenom without clinical monitoring capability is dangerous — not because 40% of patients die from it, but because 5–20% need adrenaline that an untrained CHW cannot safely administer. The causal chain to a facility with monitoring capacity remains essential regardless of ASV formulation.

Antivenom science — why formulation determines field efficacy

Venom pharmacology — why timing and dose are everything

Snake venoms are complex mixtures of proteins with different biological targets. India's "Big Four" represent the three main venom classes:

Neurotoxic (Bungarus caeruleus — common krait; Naja naja — Indian cobra)

Venom binds post-synaptic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction (α-neurotoxins) or blocks presynaptic release of acetylcholine (β-neurotoxins). Clinical presentation: progressive descending paralysis — ptosis → ophthalmoplegia → limb weakness → respiratory paralysis. Death from respiratory failure within 4–24 hours if untreated. Treatment: polyvalent ASV + mechanical ventilation + neostigmine/atropine for post-synaptic toxins. Time-critical because ASV works before toxin binds but is ineffective after binding occurs at the junction.

Hemotoxic / vasculotoxic (Daboia russelii — Russell's viper; Echis carinatus — saw-scaled viper)

Venom activates the clotting cascade, causing consumptive coagulopathy — fibrinogen is consumed, clotting factors depleted, and the patient bleeds from every wound site. Clinical: 20-Minute Whole Blood Clotting Test (20WBCT) fails (blood doesn't clot); gum bleeding, hematuria, prolonged bleeding from bite site. Treatment: polyvalent ASV + fresh frozen plasma (FFP) in severe cases; adequate dose until 20WBCT normalizes. Requires repeated dosing and clinical endpoint monitoring — the skill gap is knowing when to stop.

Cytotoxic (Naja species, some Bitis)

Local tissue necrosis from phospholipases and cytotoxins; can progress to compartment syndrome, secondary infection, and limb loss even with adequate ASV. Treatment requires early high-dose ASV + wound management. The morbidity burden from permanent disability (amputations, chronic wounds) is poorly captured in mortality-only CEAs — Africa's morbidity burden from non-fatal bites is substantial.

Why faster = better, quantified

The therapeutic window closes as venom distributes. For neurotoxic bites: ASV given within 2 hours of bite has near-complete efficacy; by 6 hours, significant neuromuscular binding has occurred and ASV reverses only circulating venom, not already-bound toxin. The observed bite-to-ASV times in poorly served rural areas (often 4–12+ hours) mean that even when patients arrive and receive ASV, clinical benefit is partial. This is why the 108 transport system — which can reduce median bite-to-ASV time — has a larger impact on outcomes than its share of the treatment pathway might suggest.

Why liquid vs. lyophilized antivenom is a ~25% field efficacy difference, not a pharmacy shelf-life question

The 25% field efficacy penalty (P10 16% / P90 35%) decomposes 3:1 between stocking/availability (the dominant channel) and molecular potency loss:

Coverage/stocking channel (~19pp)

Liquid ASV specification: stable at ≤37°C. India's rural summer ambient: 40–48°C, frequently above the spec. CHC power outages — common across all three target states — mean refrigerator temperatures cycle through 4°C and 40°C unpredictably. A CHC that nominally stocks liquid ASV may actually hold thermally compromised product. On visual inspection this is undetectable — compromised liquid ASV looks identical to potent product. The stocking failure is invisible until a patient receives a full labeled dose and the clinical response is absent.

Lyophilized ASV: ambient-stable 2–3 years at temperatures up to ~25°C standard storage; documented no-significant-potency-loss at 56°C/1 year in accelerated stability testing (PLoS NTD). It can be pre-positioned at CHCs without any cold-chain dependency, removing the stocking failure mode entirely.

Molecular potency loss (~6pp)

Toxicon Big-Four QA analysis documents that some Indian polyvalent ASV batches already fail WHO minimum potency thresholds on pre-use testing at central stores — before they ever experience field temperature excursions. The between-batch potency variation is substantial. Third-party QA is not routine; manufacturer self-certification is the norm. This potency uncertainty is additive to the stocking issue and explains the P10–P90 width of the penalty estimate.

Cost-effectiveness by tier

Tier 1 — NAPSE awareness + surveillance Median $134/DALY

The National Action Plan for Snakebite Envenomation (NAPSE, PIB 2024) provides the national policy spine. The philanthropic buy is the scale-out and evaluation of IEC: regional-language community awareness via MCBT's train-the-trainer model, master-trainer deployment, and surveillance infrastructure (notifiable-disease registration + ICMR-NIRRCH network) generating a verified CFR numerator and denominator.

Critical distinction from typical awareness programs: the target behavior is not snake-avoidance but immediate facility-seeking + 108 trigger. Awareness that produces delayed care-seeking with first-aid intervention (incision, tourniquet) is worse than no awareness. Mode-C training is explicitly conditional on a hard rule: immediate kill if any harmful first-aid taught.

Tier 2 — CHC ASV banks + ambulance transport Median $207/DALY (lyophilized ASV)

GVK-EMRI's 108 ambulance system carries ASV in 16 states and enrolled 29,461 snakebite cases in 2014. The operational backbone exists. The philanthropic buy is snakebite-specific: lyophilized ASV pre-positioning at CHCs in endemic districts (eliminating the ~19pp stocking penalty), independent batch potency QA, and 108 snakebite pathway protocol (community trigger → ambulance dispatch → potent ASV at CHC).

The $207/DALY estimate assumes lyophilized ASV at CHC level and a functional transport linkage. Under liquid ASV with real field conditions, effective coverage drops and $/DALY rises above the $150 threshold. Lyophilized procurement is not optional — it is what makes Tier 2 cost-effective.

Tier 3 — District hospital clinical QI Median $110/DALY — below $150 threshold

Guideline-concordant ASV management in typical district hospitals without training runs below 40%. The clinical gap is trainable: 20WBCT for hemotoxic endpoint monitoring, neostigmine/atropine titration for post-synaptic neurotoxic envenomation, FFP for severe coagulopathy, and mechanical ventilation readiness for respiratory paralysis. None of this requires advanced technology — it requires trained staff with access to the right supportive care equipment.

The AIIMS Patna–SHS Bihar MoU provides a Bihar-specific delivery vehicle, but requires a snakebite-specific annex with a district CFR endpoint before Tranche B release. The DH snakebite CFR target is ≤2–3% vs. 5–8% baseline — demonstrated achievable at Demow CHC (Toxicon 2025).

Africa — conditions for clearing <$150/DALY

Africa analysis — threshold conditions

Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for approximately 30,000 snakebite deaths per year — roughly 22% of the global burden. Nigeria alone likely accounts for 6,000–10,000 deaths annually. The question is whether a philanthropic snakebite portfolio in SSA can clear <$150/DALY, and under what specific conditions.

The short answer: plausible in Nigeria and Ethiopia under demanding conditions; not yet achievable at scale.

Why Africa's baseline is worse — and why that helps the CEA

In poorly served rural SSA settings where organized ASV access is absent, case fatality rates are substantially higher than India's 5–8% facility CFR. Community-level CFR for Echis envenomation in Nigeria has been documented at 10–20% in areas without reliable antivenom access; for Bitis gabonica (Gaboon viper, rarer but highly lethal), untreated CFR approaches 40–80%. A higher baseline CFR means more deaths avertable per intervention dollar — which, holding cost constant, compresses the $/DALY estimate.

The basic arithmetic for the Africa threshold: at $150/DALY and 30 DALYs/death, the cost per averted death budget is $4,500. If baseline CFR is 15% and intervention reduces to 2% (13pp reduction), then at 1,000 snakebite patients the intervention averts 130 deaths and has a total budget of $585,000 — roughly $585/patient (including those who survive without intervention). This is achievable in principle if ASV costs and delivery costs are controlled.

Most plausible Africa pathway to <$150/DALY

Nigeria — ECWA Egbe model scale-out. The ECWA Hospital Egbe in Kogi State, Nigeria documented near-zero snakebite mortality through a systematic ASV access + protocol program — the "Egbe protocol." If this model can be replicated at 20–30 rural CHC-level facilities in Nigeria's highest-burden states (Benue, Kogi, Enugu, Niger State), and if EchiTAb-Plus procurement costs can be reduced through Global Fund / Wellcome Trust volume purchasing:

  • Lyophilized EchiTAb-Plus at ≤$8/vial (requires volume purchasing at ~100k vials/yr)
  • Treatment cost ≤ $60–100 per patient (4–8 vials × $8–15 + supportive care supplies)
  • Community transport via motorcycle ambulance scheme (cost ~$30–50 per transport in Nigeria)
  • Health worker training at CHC level (~$500/health worker one-time)
  • At baseline CFR 15%, intervention CFR 2%: 13pp reduction × 1,000 bites = 130 deaths averted
  • Total cost per 1,000 patients: $100 treatment × 500 seeking care + $40 transport × 500 + training amortized ≈ $80,000–100,000
  • $/death averted: $80,000 / 130 = $615/death → $615 / 30 DALY = $20/DALY

This is a highly optimistic calculation. The EchiTAb-Plus pricing assumption ($8/vial) requires volumes that don't currently exist. The 15% baseline CFR assumes truly unserved populations. The 13pp CFR reduction requires clinical capacity that needs building. But it shows the potential: Africa's higher baseline CFR is a genuine asset that compresses $/DALY if costs are controlled.

Ethiopia has a different pathway — high Echis burden in Tigray and Amhara regions, some existing government infrastructure, and WHO/MSF presence. But the current political situation (post-Tigray conflict) makes programmatic planning uncertain.

What needs to happen before Africa clears the bar in practice

  1. EchiTAb-Plus production volume needs to increase 5–10× for pricing to fall to <$10/vial. Requires a committed buyer — government health ministry + philanthropic bridge. Lead time: 3–5 years.
  2. A community transport model for rural Africa (motorcycle ambulance, prepaid transport vouchers) needs to be proven at scale for snakebite specifically. Existing programs (Riders for Health, etc.) can be adapted but need snakebite-specific protocols.
  3. A Nigeria proof-of-concept funded by LSTMH/Wellcome/MSF at 20–30 sites with CFR endpoint — similar to the Odisha model — needs to run for 2–3 years before scale investment is justified.
  4. The thermostable next-generation antivenom pipeline (VASG, VenomAid) needs to produce a commercially scalable product — estimated 5–7 years from current Phase 1/2 stage.

Conclusion: Africa is a 3–5 year investment horizon, not a current deployment bet. The India portfolio is the right primary investment now because the infrastructure, research anchors, government engagement, and policy frameworks are in place. Fund Africa surveillance, antivenom pipeline catalysis, and a Nigeria proof-of-concept at modest scale ($2–5M) as a hedge and preparation for the Africa opportunity — not as the primary bet.

Verdict

Strategic framing (Prevail / RenPhil, Sep 2026)
Snakebite is assessed as "Prevail-shaped" if key hurdles are crossed. The proposed phased structure: Phase 1 — $10–15M test portfolio across 5 diverse areas (Odisha, Bihar, ±1 India state, Nigeria proof-of-concept, Ethiopia scoping). Phase 2 — scale post-de-risking once M12/M24 kill-switches are cleared. Co-funders Andrew and Dan want to stay informed as the program de-risks; Prevail is capacity-constrained and not actively scoping at this time. Priority parallel task: source a GM/consultant with Wellcome Trust alumni connections and comfort with underserved cause areas.

India snakebite is a delivery systems problem with a settled treatment, an operational transport network, a government policy framework, and a primary field-data evidence base. The portfolio sits at a ~$150/DALY blended estimate — at the threshold, not clearly inside it. District QI at $110/DALY is the strongest component and is well inside the bar. CHC/transport at $207/DALY is outside on raw cost but is the prerequisite for Tier 3 to function.

Africa has a plausible path to <$150/DALY — potentially much lower — but it requires EchiTAb-Plus pricing to fall 5–8× and community transport infrastructure that doesn't yet exist at scale. Fund Africa at $2–5M as a proof-of-concept and antivenom pipeline catalysis; fund India at $27M/yr as the primary bet.

The kill-switches are outcome-based and should stay that way. If M24 CFR doesn't move with CI ≠ 0, the portfolio failed. Process metrics are leading indicators, not success criteria.
