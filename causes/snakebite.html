138,000 deaths per year. A treatment that works. A delivery system that fails. An R&D pipeline that is further away than advertised. The question is whether philanthropy can fix the delivery system — and how much it costs per life saved.

What is snakebite — and why does it matter?

For readers new to the space
Snakebite is one of the largest neglected killers in global health. It is invisible in most Western philanthropic conversations — because it kills poor agricultural workers in rural South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, far from data systems and policy attention. The WHO only re-listed it as a priority neglected tropical disease in 2017 (having removed it in 2009 for "insufficient evidence"). As of 2026, WHO's 2030 roadmap target of a 50% reduction in deaths is significantly off-track.

Each year, roughly 5.4 million people are bitten by snakes, of whom 1.8–2.7 million are envenomed (receive a significant venom dose). Between 81,000 and 138,000 die, and approximately 400,000 survivors are left with permanent disability — amputations, chronic kidney disease, contractures, and psychological sequelae. The Million Death Study (Suraweera 2020) places India's toll at ~58,000 deaths per year, roughly 42% of the global burden.

These deaths are overwhelmingly preventable. An effective treatment — antivenom — has existed since the 1890s. The problem is not scientific ignorance. It is a delivery system that fails at almost every step between the bite and the treatment.

Who gets bitten

Snakebite skews male (64% of victims) and to the 30–39 age band — the prime productive years. Victims are overwhelmingly agricultural workers: paddy farmers transplanting rice in monsoon season, plantation workers clearing vegetation, field laborers moving at dusk when many snakes are active. 62% of India's bites occur during the kharif (monsoon) planting season. Most bites land on the lower limb — the ankle or foot of someone working barefoot in a paddy field, because you cannot do paddy work in boots. Most deaths occur at home or in transit, never reaching a hospital with functioning antivenom.

The poverty-death connection is direct and brutal. Families who cannot afford transport delay seeking care. Those who reach a private clinic first may spend catastrophic sums on inappropriate treatments before being referred to a public facility. Snakebite debt is a documented driver of poverty traps in rural India and sub-Saharan Africa.

The DALY structure — why it is 90% a mortality problem

Snakebite DALYs decompose roughly as follows: ~90% Years of Life Lost (YLL, from mortality), ~2% acute morbidity, and ~8% chronic sequelae (kidney disease, amputations, chronic pain, PTSD). This matters for the cost-effectiveness analysis: the primary lever for improving DALYs is preventing deaths, not managing sequelae. The chronic sequelae are real and under-counted in GBD estimates, but adding them correctly to the model only improves the $/DALY estimate by ~4% — not enough to change any investment decision. This is a mortality problem first.

How snakebite kills — the biology of envenomation

Understanding how venom kills is essential to understanding why the treatment works, why timing is everything, and why certain clinical errors are fatal. India's most dangerous snakes — the "Big Four" — represent three distinct venom mechanisms, each with its own time profile and treatment logic.

The time-efficacy curve — why faster is not just "better" but categorically different

Antivenom neutralizes only circulating, unbound venom. Once venom binds tissue targets, antivenom cannot reverse the damage. This creates a time-varying efficacy that has two distinct shapes — not one:

Antivenom efficacy E(t) by venom syndrome (central estimates from logistic fit to clinical data)

The key empirical fact: E(6h) ≈ 0.67 for neurotoxic bites but ≈ 0.83 for hemotoxic bites. Krait envenomation has a cliff at ~6 hours; Russell's viper has a long tail still partially treatable at 24h. For a Bihar/UP patient population — which is neurotoxic-skewed, because kraitbite is common in north India — the realized antivenom efficacy across all patients who reach care is approximately 0.63 (P10–P90: 0.55–0.69). This is not 1.0. The common model assumption of binary (treated = 100% effective) is wrong and inflates the cost-effectiveness estimate by roughly 1.6×.

The realized efficacy correction is the most important single fact in the CEA. The original model estimated ~$109/DALY for an India antivenom distribution program. Correcting for two factors — effective species coverage (~0.53, because Indian polyvalent ASV fails against regional venom variants) and realized time-varying efficacy (~0.63) — gives a corrected estimate of ~$335/DALY for undifferentiated distribution. The same $335/DALY result is why this portfolio funds targeted delivery (to reach-capable blocks with verified potent product and trained staff), not spray-everywhere distribution.

The treatment — what antivenom is and how it works

Antivenom (anti-snake venom, ASV) is the only proven treatment for serious snakebite envenomation. There is no oral drug, no widely available synthetic alternative, and no traditional remedy with evidence of mortality benefit. Understanding what ASV is helps explain both why it works and why it fails so frequently in field conditions.

How antivenom is made

Equine polyvalent ASV — the dominant product globally, including all of India's production — is made as follows: horses are immunized with escalating doses of venom from multiple snake species over several months. The horses develop antibodies (immunoglobulins) against the venom proteins. These antibodies are extracted from the horse's blood, purified, and concentrated into a liquid product. The antibodies circulate in a treated patient's bloodstream, bind free venom molecules, and neutralize them before they reach their target tissues.

India's "Big Four" polyvalent ASV covers venom from all four species — Naja naja, Bungarus caeruleus, Daboia russelii, Echis carinatus — meaning the horses are immunized with venom from all four. A single vial contains antibodies targeting all four venoms. But — critically — the product is only as good as the horses' immune response to the specific venom samples used in immunization, and those samples may not match the venom composition of snakes in the patient's geographic area.

Why it must be given intravenously

ASV must be administered intravenously (IV) because the antibody proteins are too large to be absorbed through the gut or skin in therapeutic quantities. This is the single biggest structural barrier to last-mile delivery: any setting that administers antivenom must have IV access capability, which means trained staff (at minimum a nurse or ANM), IV equipment, and monitoring capacity for adverse reactions. This requirement cannot be engineered away with current equine-derived products. It is the floor of clinical capability the entire portfolio is designed around.

The anaphylaxis problem — and why the numbers are often misquoted

Equine antivenom commonly triggers adverse reactions. The published range is wide, and the distinction between reaction types matters enormously for policy:

  • Any adverse reaction (fever, chills, urticaria, rash): 30–60% of patients. These are expected, manageable, and rarely life-threatening. Standard pre-treatment with paracetamol and antihistamine reduces severity.
  • True early anaphylaxis (hypotension, bronchospasm, angioedema): 5–20% depending on product and population. Requires adrenaline (epinephrine) and clinical monitoring — treatable, but dangerous without preparation.
  • Severe systemic anaphylaxis requiring ICU: ~1–3%. The genuinely dangerous tail.

The "40% adverse reaction" figure sometimes cited refers to the first category (any reaction). It is not 40% true anaphylaxis. The policy implication is precise: any site administering ASV must have epinephrine and IV access on hand. The marginal cost of pre-positioning an epinephrine kit alongside every ASV deployment is tiny (~$27/DALY incremental), and it converts the harm tail to near-zero. This is now a mandatory co-deployment in the portfolio design.

Formulation — why liquid vs. lyophilized is a ~25pp field efficacy difference

Indian polyvalent ASV is predominantly produced and distributed as a liquid formulation. Liquid ASV specification: stable at ≤37°C. India's rural summer ambient temperatures routinely reach 40–48°C. CHC power outages — endemic across Bihar, Odisha, and Jharkhand — mean refrigerators cycle through unpredictable temperature excursions. A facility that nominally stocks liquid ASV may hold thermally compromised product that appears identical to potent product on visual inspection. This invisible failure is the dominant field efficacy penalty.

Lyophilized (freeze-dried) ASV eliminates this problem entirely. Lyophilized ASV is ambient-stable for 2–3 years at up to ~25°C standard storage, with documented no-significant-potency-loss at 56°C for one year in accelerated testing (Cardoso 2017 PLoS NTD non-inferiority RCT). It can be pre-positioned at facilities without any cold-chain dependency. The switch from liquid to lyophilized ASV is estimated to eliminate roughly 19 of a 25 percentage-point field efficacy penalty — the remaining ~6pp is from molecular potency variation in the ASV itself (some Indian batches fail WHO minimum potency thresholds on pre-use testing, before any temperature exposure).

Lyophilized procurement is not optional in this portfolio. It is what makes Tier 2 cost-effective.

Why the treatment fails — the delivery gap

The ICMR National Task Force cross-sectional survey (Nat Commun 2025) is the most rigorous primary epidemiological evidence base for India snakebite. It covered 25 districts, 11 states, 60M population, and 7,094 cases. It defines precisely where the mortality occurs and where interventions can intercept it.

Where India's 58,000 annual snakebite deaths occur in the care pathway

The two leak points define the portfolio's two strategic tasks:

  • Pre-hospital deaths (~50%): Victims who never reach a facility with potent antivenom. This is the transport problem — attack it with Tier 2 (CHC ASV banks + 108 ambulance) and Tier 1 (awareness that drives immediate facility-seeking).
  • In-hospital deaths (~50%): Victims who reach care but receive wrong, insufficient, degraded, or too-late antivenom. This is the mismatch and quality problem — attack it with Tier 3 (district QI: dosing protocols, species-concordant treatment, FFP for coagulopathy).

The cold-chain failure is invisible until it kills

A Community Health Centre that nominally stocks ASV, maintains a refrigerator, and records "ASV stocked: YES" on its register may hold entirely compromised product. Liquid ASV exposed to 45°C for 48 hours during a power outage is visually indistinguishable from potent product. The patient receives a full labeled dose of ineffective antibody soup, fails to respond, and is recorded as an "antivenom failure" — when the real failure was procurement of a liquid product into an environment where cold chain cannot be guaranteed. This is the dominant, invisible failure mode, and it is exactly why lyophilized ASV procurement is non-negotiable in the portfolio design.

Independent QA failure — potency varies before the product ever leaves the warehouse

The Toxicon Big-Four QA analysis found substantial between-batch protein concentration variation in Indian polyvalent ASV, with some batches failing WHO minimum potency thresholds on pre-use testing at central stores — before any field temperature exposure. VINS batches averaged ~198mg/vial protein vs. Bharat Serums ~98mg/vial in one study. Indian ASV operates under manufacturer self-certification; third-party QA is not routine. No Indian manufacturer currently holds WHO prequalification. This batch-to-batch variability is additive to the cold-chain issue and explains why independent potency testing at 80%+ of target CHCs is a Tier 2 kill-switch criterion.

The mismatch problem — patients who seek care but still die

86% of snakebite victims in India seek hospital care. Only 60% receive antivenom — a 26-percentage-point gap. But there is a second, less visible problem: of the 60% who receive antivenom, how many receive the right product, at the right dose, in time? Systematic data is sparse, but the clinical evidence suggests the answer is: fewer than half receive guideline-concordant management.

Layer 1: Species mismatch — product covers the wrong snake

Indian polyvalent ASV covers the Big Four. But roughly 15–20% of serious envenomations in India involve species outside this panel — Trimeresurus pit vipers in the Northeast, Ophiophagus king cobra in forested areas. These patients receive the standard polyvalent, which may provide zero neutralization for the actual venom. The clinical team doesn't know: most Indian facilities lack systematic snake identification. The patient gets the treatment protocol, appears to have received antivenom, and dies anyway — recorded as "antivenom failure."

A deeper problem: even within the Big Four, the venom composition of Daboia russelii (Russell's viper) varies significantly by geography. Senji Laxme et al. 2021 showed that south India Russell's viper venom fails to protect against north India specimens in animal models. "Indian polyvalent ASV" is not uniform coverage across India's geography. Effective species coverage is estimated at ~0.53, not 1.0 — this is the species-correction multiplier that raised the undifferentiated distribution estimate from $109 to $335/DALY.

Layer 2: Dose mismatch — under-dosing is the dominant clinical error

The most common clinical error in snakebite management is not absence of antivenom but under-dosing. WHO guidelines require dosing to clinical endpoint: 20WBCT (20-Minute Whole Blood Clotting Test) normalization for hemotoxic bites; clinical paralysis reversal for neurotoxic bites. This may require 10–30+ vials over the treatment course.

In practice, many district hospitals administer a fixed "starter" dose of 8–10 vials and stop — because they're rationing limited stock, because the clinical team doesn't know to continue, or because the patient refuses further treatment after partial improvement. Guideline-concordant dosing occurs at fewer than 50% of district hospitals. Under-dosing in hemotoxic bites leads to ongoing coagulopathy, delayed bleeding, and death hours after discharge — deaths recorded as community deaths rather than in-facility antivenom failures, which disappear from the statistics.

Layer 3: Timing mismatch — antivenom given, but past the therapeutic window

For neurotoxic bites (Bungarus, Naja), ASV is effective only against circulating free venom. Once β-bungarotoxin binds and destroys the nerve terminal, antivenom cannot reverse it. The therapeutic window is effectively ≤6 hours for krait envenomation. Rural bite-to-facility times in Bihar and Odisha routinely exceed 4–6 hours.

Patients who arrive late, receive full-dose antivenom correctly, and are recorded as "received antivenom" may still die from respiratory failure — not because antivenom doesn't work, but because it was given past the point of reversibility. This is why transport improvement (108 ambulance dispatch) has a DALY impact disproportionate to its modest cost: it shifts patients from the post-threshold zone into the 1–3h window where antivenom is actually effective.

What has been tried and learned — the dead ends matter

Three decades of snakebite intervention produce both confirmed approaches and clear dead ends. Understanding the dead ends prevents wasting money on plausible-sounding interventions with no mortality impact.

  • Protective footwear (gaiters, boots) — $254,000–$1.1M/DALY. Clear decline. Snake boots cost $40–250 and mechanically work on the bench — denim alone cuts venom delivery ~60–66%. But three insurmountable problems: (1) you must pay for an entire agricultural population but only save a tiny fraction who would have had a fatal lower-limb bite in the field; (2) baseline boot-wearing among at-risk paddy workers is ~8% and heat compliance is near-zero (boots in 40°C paddy work are not tolerable for 8–10 hour days); (3) no controlled trial has ever measured a reduction in snakebite incidence from footwear distribution. Across 200,000 Monte Carlo draws, the probability of gaiters clearing $150/DALY is exactly zero. Even an optimistic steel-man corner (most snakebite-dense district on Earth, viper-dominated species mix, near-free product) lands at ~$9,000/DALY — 60× above the bar. Fund treatment reach, not boots.
  • CHW-carried ASV without clinical monitoring — net-harmful in some scenarios. Multiple programs have trained community health workers to carry ASV and administer it in the field. These fail consistently: (a) liquid ASV requires cold chain CHWs cannot maintain; (b) anaphylaxis occurs in 5–20% of patients and requires adrenaline and IV airway management that an untrained CHW cannot safely provide. CHW-carried ASV without monitoring capability can kill through anaphylaxis as often as it saves through early treatment. The causal chain to a facility with monitoring remains essential regardless of ASV formulation.
  • Mobile snake identification apps — not actionable, potentially harmful. Apps allowing bystanders to photograph the snake and receive species identification and treatment guidance failed in field conditions: poor rural connectivity, identification requires a clear photo of the snake during chaos following a bite, and even correct identification doesn't change first-line management without ASV on site. None showed mortality impact; some delayed transport while identification was attempted.
  • Community awareness without end-to-end system — zero mortality effect. The most replicated null finding in the snakebite literature. Programs teaching communities to recognize snakebite and seek care, without ensuring the facility they reach has potent, properly stored antivenom and trained staff, produce zero mortality improvement. This has been shown across India, Africa, and Southeast Asia. Demand is not the binding constraint. The target behavior for awareness programs must be immediate 108 trigger + facility-seeking, not snake avoidance or first-aid intervention.
  • Tourniquet de-training — important but slow. Traditional first-aid in South Asia includes tight tourniquet application, which causes tissue necrosis and dramatically worsens outcomes for cytotoxic bites, and delays transport. WHO and government programs are making progress, but embedded traditional practice changes over decades, not years. Worth continuing; cannot be the primary intervention.
  • Generic village ASV community banks — $1,789/DALY, 9% net-harmful. Stocking antivenom at the village level without adrenaline kits, trained staff, or monitoring capacity produces adverse outcomes more reliably than it prevents deaths. The break-even threshold is approximately 13 envenomings per year per facility minimum, with adrenaline co-deployment mandatory. Below that threshold, generic community banks are a net harm.

Who is doing what — the global treatment ecosystem

The Wellcome Trust £80M programme and the Fav-Afrique collapse

The central market failure in snakebite is identical to the AMR problem: antivenom generates insufficient commercial revenue to justify production at volumes needed for public health impact. The crisis became acute in 2014 when Sanofi Pasteur discontinued Fav-Afrique — the only polyvalent antivenom covering the full range of common African species (the "Big Four": Echis saw-scaled vipers, Naja cobras, Bitis arietans puff adder, Dendroaspis mambas). Fav-Afrique had been sold at a loss for years; when Sanofi exited, the African market lost its most effective product. Existing stocks ran out by 2016.

In May 2019, Wellcome announced an £80M, 7-year investment (2019–2026) — the dominant philanthropic actor in snakebite globally. Wellcome's portfolio is almost entirely R&D: oral/IV varespladib (sPLA₂ inhibitor) clinical trials, recombinant monoclonal antibody platforms (DTU Copenhagen, UCB), the LSTMH multi-centre African antivenom trial, and WHO Target Product Profile development. Wellcome is not funding delivery. The India portfolio in this investment case and Wellcome's programme are essentially non-overlapping. As of 2026, Wellcome is winding down its snakebite work — making Wellcome alumni a priority talent pool for a programme manager hire.

Tier 1 — NAPSE awareness + surveillance Median $134/DALY

The National Action Plan for Snakebite Envenomation (NAPSE, PIB 2024) provides the national policy spine. The philanthropic buy is the scale-out and evaluation of community education: regional-language IEC via MCBT's train-the-trainer model and surveillance infrastructure (notifiable-disease registration, ICMR-NIRRCH network) generating a verified CFR numerator and denominator.

Critical distinction from typical awareness programs: the target behavior is not snake avoidance or first-aid intervention, but immediate 108 trigger + facility-seeking. Awareness that teaches harmful first-aid (tourniquet application, incision) is worse than no awareness. Mode-C training has a hard rule: immediate kill if any harmful first-aid is included.

Tier 2 — CHC ASV banks + ambulance transport Median $207/DALY (lyophilized ASV)

GVK-EMRI's 108 ambulance system operates in 16 states and enrolled 29,461 snakebite cases in 2014. The backbone exists. The philanthropic buy is snakebite-specific: lyophilized ASV pre-positioning at CHCs in endemic districts (eliminating the ~19pp stocking penalty), independent batch potency QA, and 108 snakebite pathway protocols.

The $207/DALY estimate requires lyophilized ASV. Under liquid ASV with real field conditions, effective coverage drops and $/DALY rises above $150. The lyophilized switch is not optional — it is what makes this tier cost-effective. Above-break-even siting rule: ASV only at facilities with ≥13 envenomings/year and adrenaline on site.

Tier 3 — District hospital clinical QI Median $110/DALY — strongest component, clearly inside bar

Guideline-concordant ASV management at typical district hospitals without training runs below 40%. The clinical gap is trainable: 20WBCT for hemotoxic endpoint monitoring (knowing when to stop dosing), neostigmine/atropine titration for post-synaptic neurotoxic envenomation, FFP for severe coagulopathy, and mechanical ventilation readiness for respiratory paralysis. None of this requires advanced technology — it requires trained staff with correct supportive care equipment.

The Demow CHC proof-of-concept (Toxicon 2025) demonstrates zero snakebite deaths over 4 years in a rural Indian CHC through protocol-concordant management. The DH target is ≤2–3% CFR versus 5–8% baseline — achievable. The AIIMS Patna–SHS Bihar MoU provides a Bihar vehicle, but requires a snakebite-specific annex before any budget is committed.

Africa — conditions for clearing <$150/DALY

Africa analysis — threshold conditions

Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for approximately 30,000 snakebite deaths per year (~22% of global burden). Nigeria alone: 6,000–10,000 deaths annually. The question is whether Africa can clear <$150/DALY under what conditions.

Plausible in Nigeria and Ethiopia under demanding conditions not yet met. Africa is a 3–5 year horizon, not a current deployment bet.

Why Africa's baseline actually helps the CEA — and what still blocks it

Community-level CFR for Echis envenomation in Nigeria without reliable antivenom access has been documented at 10–20%; in areas without organized ASV access at all, this rises further. A higher baseline CFR means more deaths avertable per intervention dollar — at the same cost, Africa's higher baseline compresses $/DALY substantially.

But Africa's delivery costs are also higher: no 108-equivalent system, road quality worse, distances greater, CHC clinical capacity thinner. The EchiTAb-Plus-ICP antivenom covering West African species runs ~$40–80/vial × 4–8 vials = $160–640 per treatment course at current prices — too expensive to clear the Africa bar. Clearing the bar requires:

  • EchiTAb-Plus procurement at ≤$8–10/vial (requires volume purchasing at ~100k vials/yr — 3–5× current production)
  • A Nigeria proof-of-concept at 20–30 rural CHC-level sites (the ECWA Egbe protocol as the model) with CFR endpoint data
  • Community transport infrastructure (motorcycle ambulance schemes) proven for snakebite specifically
  • Thermostable next-generation antivenom (VASG, VenomAid pipeline) — 5–7 years from current Phase 1/2

Fund Africa at $2–5M as a proof-of-concept and antivenom pipeline catalyst. Fund India at $27M/yr as the primary bet. Do not conflate them.

Africa $/DALY arithmetic — what has to be true for the threshold to clear

Worked arithmetic for Nigeria (optimistic)

Basic setup: at $150/DALY threshold and 30 DALYs/death → $4,500 budget per averted death. At baseline CFR 15% and intervention CFR 2% (13pp reduction) across 1,000 snakebite patients → 130 deaths averted → total cost budget $585,000.

  • Lyophilized EchiTAb-Plus at ≤$8/vial (requires volume purchasing) × 6 vials = $48 per treatment
  • 500 patients seeking care × $48 treatment = $24,000
  • Transport × 500 = $40/transport × 500 = $20,000
  • CHC training amortized = $20,000–40,000
  • Total: ~$64,000–84,000 for 130 deaths averted → ~$490–650/death → ~$16–22/DALY

This is highly optimistic. The $8/vial price requires volumes that don't exist. The 15% baseline CFR assumes truly unserved populations. The 13pp CFR reduction requires clinical capacity needing investment. But it shows the potential: Africa's higher baseline CFR is a genuine asset if costs are controlled.

Verdict

Strategic framing (Prevail / RenPhil, Sep 2026)
Snakebite is assessed as "Prevail-shaped" if key hurdles are crossed. The proposed phased structure: Phase 1 — $10–15M test portfolio across 5 diverse areas (Odisha, Bihar, ±1 India state, Nigeria proof-of-concept, Ethiopia scoping). Phase 2 — scale post-de-risking once M12/M24 kill-switches are cleared. Co-funders Andrew and Dan want to stay informed as the program de-risks; Prevail is capacity-constrained and not actively scoping at this time. Highest-priority parallel task: source a GM/consultant with Wellcome Trust alumni connections.

India snakebite is a delivery systems problem with a settled treatment, an operational transport network, a government policy framework, and a primary field-data evidence base. The portfolio sits at ~$150/DALY blended — at the threshold, not clearly inside it. District QI at $110/DALY is the strongest component and is well inside the bar. CHC/transport at $207/DALY is outside on raw cost but is the prerequisite for Tier 3 to function: a trained district hospital without patients who arrived with potent ASV in their bloodstream in time cannot demonstrate a CFR reduction.

The three things this portfolio is not: (1) It is not an R&D bet — the pipeline is 4+ years from deployment readiness and P(disruption <10yr) ≈ 4%. (2) It is not undifferentiated antivenom distribution — the corrected model shows $335/DALY for generic spray-everywhere programs, worse than cash. (3) It is not a footwear program — gaiters are $254,000/DALY at the median and never clear the bar in any Monte Carlo draw.

Africa has a plausible path but demanding conditions. EchiTAb-Plus pricing must fall 5–10× and community transport infrastructure must be proven at scale. Fund Africa at $2–5M as a proof-of-concept; fund India at $27M/yr as the primary bet. Don't conflate them.

The kill-switches are outcome-based and must stay that way. If M24 CFR doesn't move with CI ≠ 0, the portfolio failed. There is no process-metric success.
