Evaluation — Sep 2026

Passage Africa Pathways

$50M loan facility for African LPN students in Canada. 3,200 loans over 5 years at 11.95% APR, recycled into new cohorts. Proposed by Renaissance Philanthropy / AJF. 6 deep-research runs completed Sep 22, 2026.

Provisional verdict

Fund with conditions — the investment case is real but the model is wrong about what makes it work.

The CE case does not depend on 165 DCYs/migrant. It depends on loan repayment. If loans repay at ≤32.5% default, capital comes back with surplus at any defensible DCY estimate. The philanthropic value is the recycling structure, not the benefit accounting. At realistic DCYs (~103 central) and realistic defaults (~15% central), every scenario except correlated systemic failure (immigration policy collapse) clears the GiveWell bar.

Three findings require grant conditions before capital is committed: (1) the 85% stay-rate assumption is indefensible — central is ~50%, which would raise $/DCY by ~62% and potentially break the case at higher default rates; (2) the Year 2 cash crunch is real and structurally preventable with a simple interest-only deferral; (3) the company has no ring-fenced SPV, no audited financials, and no board of directors — standard exposure for a startup, but unacceptable for a $50M philanthropic commitment.

The investment model

Passage lends CAD $60K per student at 11.95% APR over 7 years (CAD $1,058/month) to students from Uganda, Malawi, Burundi, and Madagascar pursuing a 20-month Licensed Practical Nurse diploma at accredited Canadian colleges. Renaissance proposes a $50M award ($30M from AJF + $20M co-funders), funding ~3,200 students over 5 years. Repayments are recycled into new cohorts.

The financial structure is unusually self-hedging: the 11.95% APR generates CAD $28,872 in interest per performing loan, which absorbs defaults up to ~32.5% before the capital pool begins to shrink. At the model's base case (10% default), capital returns with a 41% surplus. This is the structural advantage the evaluation turns on.

The CE model claims 165 DCYs/migrant — weighted average across four countries — at near-zero net cost. (A DCY, or Doubling of Consumption Year, means one person's annual consumption doubled for one year. It's GiveWell's standard unit for measuring economic impact from income-lifting programs.) 75% of that benefit is claimed to flow to origin communities via remittances and spillover — both disputed.

The key insight from six research runs: This is a bet on loan repayment, not on DCY magnitude. At realistic DCYs (~103) and realistic defaults (~15%), the money still comes back with a 26% surplus — meaning the program has negative net cost at realistic defaults. The case breaks only if defaults exceed ~42% — which requires correlated systemic failure, not normal credit deterioration.

The remittance critique: Statistics Canada's SIMT survey (2017) shows SSA remitters in Canada average 4.5% of $60K income ($2,710/yr). The LSIC (Longitudinal Survey of Immigrants to Canada, 2008) shows 7.5% in year 2 declining to 5.9% by year 4. Malengo uses 8% (already below Passage's 9%). A realistic lifetime average: ~7% weighted at 4% discount. Additionally, the "2 households" assumption is generous — effective coverage is ~1.3 equivalent households. Loan repayment at 11.95% (~$5–8K/yr in debt service early in the loan) directly competes with remittance capacity.

The spillover critique: The 1.65× multiplier transplants Egger et al. (2022) — a concentrated one-time cash transfer injecting >15% of local GDP into 653 rural Kenyan villages — to dispersed monthly remittances of ~$450 to 1–2 urban/peri-urban households across four different countries over 40 years. GiveWell's own adjustments for Uganda/Malawi imply an effective spillover addition of only ~0.10–0.13×, not 0.65×. Revised central: ~1.25×.

The CE implication: At near-zero net cost (loans recycled), ALL scenarios clear the GiveWell bar. The DCY reduction matters primarily if defaults are high enough to create positive net cost. At $5K net cost per migrant (modest default losses): revised central ($49/DCY) just barely exceeds GiveWell's $40/DCY bar. The margin of safety is thin.

Reframe recommendation
The honest case is: "a large, robust income gain for the migrant (~$44 DCYs defensible floor) with modest and uncertain community spillover." The migrant's own income gain is the load-bearing asset. The community benefit story (75% of value to origin households) is the weak part. Lead with the revolving fund economics, not the DCY headline.

Default rate analysis

Run: Passage_default_rate_comparable_lenders — 3 phases, Sep 22 2026

No true comparable exists: Passage combines $60K unsecured loans at 11.95% for SSA (Sub-Saharan African) international students with no government backstop, no income-contingent repayment, no grace period, and payments beginning from Month 1. Every comparable lender with <10% default has at least one of these protective features; Passage has none.

Central default estimate: ~15% (range 12–19%), above Passage's 10% base case. Derived by starting from Canada's 6.7–8% domestic student loan benchmark and applying risk adjustments:

  • No income-based repayment / no deferral during study: +5–10pp
  • $60K loan size (6× Windmill, 2–3× OSAP average): +2–3pp
  • 11.95% APR vs 0–6.7% for comparables: +1–2pp
  • No grace period + full payment during study: +3–5pp
  • Cross-border collection risk for non-completers: +1–2pp
  • Near-certain nursing employment (downward): −5–8pp
  • Strong migration motivation / positive self-selection (downward): −2–3pp

The structural reason: The 11.95% APR generates $28,872 in surplus per performing loan. Each default consumes $60,000 in principal. Performing-loan surpluses absorb defaults until one-third of the portfolio fails — a self-hedging revolving loan. Default is NOT the right reason to decline the investment. The case fails, if at all, on stay-rate (see below) or systemic immigration risk — not on credit defaults.

The Year 2 mechanism: The Year 2 clinical placement creates a payment/income ratio of 102–203% (payment of $1,058 on $520–1,039 monthly income). This is the discrete mechanism by which 10% becomes 15–20% — not because graduates can't eventually afford the payments, but because a 6–8 month forced-arrears period during clinical creates compounding interest and normalized non-payment that some fraction never escapes.

LPN income outcomes

Run: Passage_LPN_income_international_graduates — 3 phases, Sep 22 2026

The $60K base case is defensible as a Year 2–3 full-time estimate but optimistic as a Year 1 expected-value figure. The model's $60K sits in the middle of the new-grad LPN collective-agreement wage grid ($56–62K annualized at $27–30/hr). The national LPN median is $65.1K — slightly above the model's base.

Critical distinction: Passage graduates are Canadian-educated international students, not internationally-educated nurses (IENs) arriving with foreign credentials. 86–93% of Canadian-educated immigrant nurses hold health occupations matching their education, vs only 42–50% for foreign-educated IENs. The Health Canada IEHP mean of $56,500 is the WRONG benchmark — it includes foreign-educated, underemployed, and part-time workers. The correct benchmark is the new-grad LPN wage grid ($56–62K FT).

The $60K base case is approximately the Year 3 probability-weighted income, not the Year 1 expected value. African-origin specific: African-origin immigrant nurses show above-average nursing employment: 70.2% in nursing occupations vs 63% for all immigrant nurses (StatCan Sep 2025). LPN-specific alignment: 74%. However, a documented racial wage penalty (~5–10% effective income penalty through hours and scheduling, not hourly rate) applies in the Canadian nursing context. Union collective agreements partially mitigate this at the wage level.

Year 2 cash crunch

Run: Passage_year2_affordability_student_protection — 3 phases, Sep 22 2026

The Year 2 cash crunch is real, severe, and the single most likely point of failure in Passage's loan-and-graduation model. The concept note's CAD $56/month residual before food is an optimistic ceiling that requires continuous 20-month disbursement, $500/month rent, and zero miscellaneous spending.

Whether living-cost disbursement runs 12 or 20 months is the single most important factual question for the CE model. Passage's FAQ states disbursement is "12: once a month for a year." If this means disbursement stops when clinical placement begins, the Year 2 problem is not a budget crunch — it is structurally impossible without external support, and the model's default adjustment should be +10–15pp (total 20–25% default rate), not +3pp.

Every comparator lender offers protection during study: Canada Student Loans charge $0 during study with a 6-month grace period; Windmill charges interest-only during study (up to 27 months); MPOWER charges interest-only with 24 months of forbearance. Passage is the sole exception among comparable lenders.

Recommended structural fix

Interest-only payments during 8-month clinical placement: This frees CAD $519/month per student, costs Passage only ~$4,150 in temporarily deferred principal (fully recovered post-graduation), is revenue-positive for Passage (more total interest), and pays for itself if it prevents just 1 dropout per ~6.4 students offered the deferral. This is the single highest-ROI structural fix.

Canada PR pathway reliability

Run: Passage_Canada_PR_pathway_reliability — 3 phases, Sep 22 2026

Most important model error
The 85% stay-rate assumption is not defensible. Central estimate ~50% (band 35–65%), well below the model's assumption. At 50% instead of 85%, the model overstates income-based DCYs by ~38% (1.62× $/DCY). This is a top-3 CE parameter and interacts multiplicatively with income gain, default rate, and program cost.

Three independent methods converge on ~44–54%:

  • Bottom-up 7-gate chain: 44% — diploma completion × REx-PN pass × PGWP × employment × PNP nomination (Provincial Nominee Program — Canada's main route where provinces sponsor skilled workers for permanent residency) × PR approval × chooses to stay
  • Adjusted StatCan healthcare TFW anchor: 54% — StatCan 2025 shows 58% of healthcare TFWs from 2000–2022 obtained Canadian PR; haircut −4pp for immigration retrenchment
  • Scenario-weighted (including correlated tail): 45%

The PNP gate is the decisive bottleneck. A typical Passage LPN graduate scores ~330–380 CRS points (Comprehensive Ranking System — Canada's points-based immigration score combining age, education, language, and work experience). Healthcare Express Entry draws in 2026 required 462–476 — the gap is unbridgeable without a PNP nomination (+600 CRS). PNP IS the pathway. If PNP allocation is exhausted or streams are paused, the student has no viable PR route.

Ontario PNP reached its 2025 allocation in December 2025. Ontario then terminated all 8 OINP streams on May 30, 2026 and replaced them with a single Workforce Priority stream (June 26, 2026) — with healthcare-specific rules not yet published as of September 2026. This is live regulatory uncertainty for Passage's 2026–2028 cohorts.

At 50% stay rate combined with 15% default, $/DCY rises to ~$80–90 — still below the GiveWell bar ($40/DCY is the bar using published 165 DCYs). But at 50% stay + revised 103 DCYs + 15% default, the CE lands at ~$100–120/DCY — borderline. The combination of DCY revision + stay-rate revision is the case that is most likely to break GiveWell-competitiveness.

Default risk is correlated with stay-rate risk — students who fail to obtain PR are the same students who default. The model should not treat these as independent.

Company risk and financial health

Run: Passage_company_risk_financial_health — 3 phases, Sep 22 2026

Company risk is high but structurally fixable — standard investment safeguards, imposed before capital commitment, address all of it.

Passage is a 3.5-year-old VC-backed startup founded by Martin Basiri (co-founder of ApplyBoard, $1.4B+ valuation). The company raised C$40M seed funding led by Drive Capital. It has no disclosed board of directors, no published audited financials, no prudential regulator, and approximately 2 years of operating runway. At $50M portfolio scale, the net margin is ~$0–1.5M/yr — barely enough to operate, with no cushion against a bad year.

P(organizational failure within 5 years): ~25–40%, driven by thin margins, single-founder dependency, unseasoned portfolio, and PGWP (Post-Graduation Work Permit — the authorization that lets international graduates stay in Canada to work after finishing their degree) correlated risk.

The SPV transforms the risk from "will this startup survive?" to "will these loans repay?" — a much easier question to answer and control for. Without the SPV, Renaissance's $30M is unsecured exposure to a startup. With it, Renaissance owns the loan portfolio directly and Passage is merely the servicer.

Grant conditions (non-negotiable)

Twelve conditions based on six research runs. Require all before committing capital:

Structural (prevent catastrophic loss)

  1. Ring-fenced SPV: All $50M deployed through a dedicated Special Purpose Vehicle with Renaissance as primary beneficiary. Passage operates as servicer. (Single most important condition.)
  2. Backup servicer agreement: Standby contract with Nelnet Canada or equivalent to step in if Passage cannot service.
  3. Priority waterfall: Documented payment priority: borrower payments → servicing costs → default reserve → Renaissance capital return → Passage margin.

Student protection (reduce default risk)

  1. Confirm 20-month disbursement: Require Passage to confirm in writing whether living-cost disbursement runs 12 or 20 months. If 12 months, extending to 20 is a non-negotiable condition at zero additional cost to Passage.
  2. Interest-only payments during clinical placement: Require interest-only (not full P+I) during the 8-month clinical placement period. Saves students CAD $519/month at ~$4,150 temporary deferral per student — revenue-positive for Passage through extended amortization.
  3. Provincial diversification: Require Passage to place students across ≥3 provinces to diversify PNP allocation risk. Single-province concentration (Ontario only) maximizes correlated immigration risk.

Governance and transparency

  1. Annual audit: Both Passage Inc. and Passage (Funding) Inc. audited annually, delivered within 90 days of year-end.
  2. Quarterly portfolio reporting: Delinquency by vintage, defaults/write-offs, cash position, PGWP/employment outcomes — within 30 days of quarter-end.
  3. Performance triggers: If 90+ day delinquency exceeds 10%, new disbursements pause. If cumulative write-offs exceed 5%, Renaissance can halt further commitments.
  4. Board observer seat: Renaissance representative attends all board meetings with full information access.
  5. Key-person provision: If Martin Basiri ceases as CEO, Renaissance can pause further disbursements until a successor is approved.
  6. Minimum operating reserve: Passage maintains at least 12 months of operating expenses in cash reserves, separate from portfolio capital.
