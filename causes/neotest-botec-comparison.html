NeoTest Diligence · September 2026

Two BOTECs, One Grant

Akhil's model, properly computed on a marginal basis, says $119/DALY. Ours says $111. An $8 gap — not the larger numbers sometimes cited. This page explains what drives that gap, and where the two models genuinely agree.
Bottom line

Both models support the investment. On a properly marginal basis — netting out tests that would have happened anyway in a delayed counterfactual — Akhil's model gives $119/DALY and ours gives $111. Both are well inside a reasonable philanthropic bar ($100–200/DALY). The $8 gap between them is not a reason to doubt the recommendation.

The one number that matters most is not in either model: whether first-line ampicillin-plus-gentamicin actually works for the Klebsiella cases that dominate LMIC neonatal sepsis. BARNARDS 2021 found only 25–28% first-line coverage of Gram-negative isolates. Neither model applies a resistance haircut to the mortality stream — which carries ~81% of modelled DALYs in both models. That is the shared blind spot.

What is a BOTEC, and why did we build our own?

A BOTEC — back-of-the-envelope calculation — is a model that takes a set of assumptions about the world and produces a single number: how much does it cost to avert one DALY? The number is not the point. The assumptions are.

Akhil built his model for this funder — to size the social return and stress-test it against conservative scenarios. His model produces two CE numbers depending on how you handle the counterfactual:

Georgia ran Akhil's model with conservative adjustments: 4% discount rate, 7-year counterfactual launch delay, same adoption trajectory in the counterfactual, and halved peak penetration (India 30% → 15%). The Counterfactual tab (cell C71) gives $119/DALY — the right comparison to our $111.

We built an independent model starting from scratch. Our structural choices:

  • 9% cost discount rate (our portfolio opportunity cost) vs Akhil's 2% social rate or Georgia's 4%.
  • Community-adherence haircut (c2 = 0.38, calibrated to WHO PSBI programme field data) applied to the HTA preprint's DALYs-per-test.
  • Launch year updated to 2033 based on regulatory pathway analysis (India first meaningful public-sector uptake, not first commercial sale).
  • India peak adoption 5.5% (HB cluster) / 15% (Leader cluster) / 8% (private) — more conservative than Akhil's original 30%, though similar to Georgia's halved 15%.

None of these are corrections of errors. They are different lenses. The relevant comparison is $119 (Akhil marginal, conservative) vs $111 (ours). The $8 gap is explained below.

The five structural differences

1 — Discount rate: 2% vs 9% Works in both directions
Akhil

2% social rate in base model (Summary tab; PV costs: $52.6M). Georgia's conservative run (the $119 CE) uses 4%. Both contrast with our 9% opportunity-cost rate.

Ours

9% cost discount (funder opportunity cost), 4% DALY discount. "What else could this $60M earn?" PV costs: $21.2M.

The discount rate is not a technical parameter — it is a philosophical one. It answers the question: how much less do you value a dollar of impact that arrives in 2040 versus 2030?

A social planner — someone optimizing for collective welfare across time — uses a low rate (2–3%) because future people's lives matter nearly as much as present ones. A funder with an investment portfolio uses a higher rate (8–10%) because the alternative use of that money is a portfolio earning real returns.

The counterintuitive effect: our higher cost-discount rate actually makes our PV costs lower ($21.2M vs Akhil's $52.6M at 2%). Money disbursed as AMC payments in 2035–2045 is worth much less today at 9% than at 2%. But this doesn't improve our CE much, because we also model fewer DALYs (see differences 3 and 4). The cost-discount effect is more than offset by the adoption and DALYs differences.

Who is right? For comparing to GiveWell benchmarks — which use a similar opportunity-cost framing — our 9% is the right rate. For a policy audience arguing for government funding, Akhil's 2% is appropriate. Both can be correct for their intended audience.

2 — What population does the test reach? (TAM definition) Closer than it looks
Akhil

PSBI presentations: all neonates meeting WHO danger-sign criteria, both facility-born and community-presenting. India: ~1.9M/yr. Denominator includes community cases that haven't yet reached a testing facility.

Ours

SNCU admissions: neonates already in specialist wards. India: 1.9M headline, 265–500k functional (workup-relevant). The TAM is already inside the facility.

At first glance these look like very different populations. In practice, the headline TAM figures are almost identical — both are roughly 1.9M for India. That is not a coincidence: SNCU admissions and PSBI presentations in India are closely related, because ~89% of births are institutional (NFHS-5), and most PSBI cases present to or through the same facilities.

The conceptual difference matters more than the numerical one. Akhil's PSBI frame asks "how many neonates could benefit if this test were widely deployed?" Ours asks "how many are already in a facility where the test could be run today?" The PSBI frame is broader and more appropriate for long-run market sizing; the SNCU frame is more conservative and more appropriate for near-term CE estimation.

Where the two definitions diverge materially: Akhil includes community-presenting neonates who are not in SNCUs. These require both a community-level test deployment AND a functional referral pathway to get treated. Our model captures this separately through the c2 community-adherence parameter — which Akhil's model does not apply (see Difference 4).

3 — How fast does a new diagnostic spread? (Adoption rates) Akhil more optimistic → his CE better
Akhil

India peak penetration: 15% of PSBI TAM (Georgia's conservative version; Akhil's original model used 30%). 2 years to 50% of peak. Calibrated to malaria RDT rollout (~48% of febrile U5s in Africa by 2017).

Ours

India HB cluster: 5.5%; Leader cluster: 15%; private: 8%. Calibrated to TNMSC/Karnataka tender histories for CRP and blood-culture kit procurement.

This is a large driver of the gap. Note: the 15% figure in the model we reviewed is Georgia's halved value — Akhil's original model used 30% India peak adoption. Sep 2026 clarification from Akhil: the 30% figure represents 30% of facility births that develop neonatal danger signs — not 30% of all births. The denominator includes PHCs, birthing clinics, and subdistrict hospitals that see neonatal danger signs but lack an SNCU. This is meaningfully different from our SNCU-only TAM. Akhil argues SNCU is a genuine lower bound because the WHO TPP explicitly targets non-SNCU deployment (non-hospital operating point: 90% Se / 70% Sp), and a lateral-flow POCT is deployable at PHC level in a way GeneXpert was not.

Our model's blended 5.5–15% across clusters generates roughly 130,000 India tests/year at peak; Akhil's original (PHC-inclusive TAM) generates up to ~570,000. Georgia's halved conservative version ≈ 285,000. India carries ~50% of all modelled DALYs, so this divergence is the single largest driver of the CE gap. Whether PHC-level deployment is achievable — electricity, training, test interpretation, supply chain — is an open empirical question. A loop on PHC TAM feasibility is running.

Sep 2026 call clarification: Akhil uses a dual analog, not just malaria RDT. GeneXpert calibrates the absolute penetration level (more conservative — instrument-based, molecular, requires training and cold chain). Malaria RDT calibrates the slope steepness (rate of ramp-up). The SNCU procurement history is retained as a lower-bound triangulation. This is a more defensible methodology than single-analog — GeneXpert is the right reference for a specialist clinical deployment context.

Even so, NeoTest is simpler than GeneXpert (lateral-flow vs molecular, ambient vs cold-chain storage), which argues for faster adoption curves than GeneXpert achieved. A research loop on the GeneXpert penetration curve — time to 50% peak in India, Nigeria, Ethiopia — is running to validate whether Akhil's penetration parameters are calibrated correctly.

Our calibration to TNMSC and Karnataka tender histories is more specific but less data-rich. The honest range is wide. Akhil's 15% (Georgia conservative) is not implausible over 15 years; our 5.5% for the HB cluster is conservative but appropriate as the starting point for a novel diagnostic without prior SNCU procurement precedent.

Why 5.5% rather than 15%? The India SNCU procurement reality

India's 1.9M SNCU admissions headline includes a long tail of non-functional units — facilities that technically have an SNCU designation but run without adequate staffing, power, or supplies. The workup-relevant denominator (where a clinician would actually use a rapid test result) is closer to 265–500k.

5.5% of the headline 1.9M is 105k tests — which is 21–40% of the corrected functional TAM. In that framing, 5.5% is not conservative; it is ambitious relative to current Indian public-sector CRP testing rates, which run below 10% even in well-equipped SNCUs.

The Leader cluster (AIIMS, JIPMER, KEM) runs at 15% adoption in our model — closer to Akhil's assumption — because these institutions already have functioning microbiological workup protocols and are the most likely early adopters. The difference is that we do not assume Leader-tier adoption generalises rapidly to the HB cluster of district hospitals.

4 — Does community deployment actually work? (DALYs per test) Akhil more optimistic → his CE better
Akhil

0.1665 DALYs/test, taken directly from the HTA preprint. The HTA models a 50/50 inborn/community split with its own internal referral and adherence rates baked in.

Ours

~0.132 DALYs/test after applying c2 = 0.38 community adherence haircut. WHO PSBI programme field data calibrates the referral-adherence central at 0.38, not the 0.50 the HTA implies.

The HTA's community DALY estimate rests on a specific assumption about how often a positive test result in a community setting actually leads to treatment. The HTA uses a referral rate parameter (Ref_M = 30%, Ref_H = 60%) that implies an effective community adherence of roughly 0.45–0.50.

When we calibrate this parameter to WHO PSBI programme field data, the empirical central is 0.38 — about 24% below the HTA's implied 0.50. Since the community arm carries roughly half of modelled DALYs, the effect on total DALYs/test is about 12%: from 0.1665 (Akhil, no c2 adjustment) to approximately 0.132 in our model after applying c2 = 0.38. This is a 21% reduction relative to Akhil's 0.1665.

This is the most debatable difference. Akhil's HTA is the only published quantitative framework for this technology — it is the natural starting point. Our c2 haircut is a judgment that real-world community adherence in LMIC settings runs lower than the HTA's parameters assume. Both positions can be defended. The empirical answer will require field data from early deployments.

What c2 actually means — and why it matters so much

c2 is the community adherence parameter: the fraction of test-positive infants in community settings who actually receive the treatment the test result recommends. It captures everything between "the test gives a positive result" and "the baby gets antibiotics": does the health worker tell the family? Does the family believe it? Can they get to a facility? Does the facility have stock?

In the 50/50 inborn/community model, c2 only applies to the community half. The inborn (SNCU) half has structural adherence near 1.0 — the clinician is already there, the baby is already admitted, the antibiotics are already available.

Moving c2 from 0.50 (HTA implied) to 0.38 (WHO PSBI empirical central) reduces the community mortality stream by about 24%, which cuts total DALYs/test from 0.1665 to approximately 0.132 — a 21% reduction. This is a significant driver of our higher CE versus Akhil's.

After the care-seeking chain analysis we conducted, we confirmed that c2 (clinician/family adherence to test result) is now the binding constraint — not L1/L2 (care-seeking and reaching a facility), which the denominator correction showed was a wrong-denominator artifact. The question is not whether PSBI babies reach a testing facility; in India, 89% of births are institutional. The question is whether a positive result at 48–72 hours changes the treatment decision. Field evidence from Indian NICUs is promising but limited to a few sites.

5 — When does the test reach market? (Launch year) Akhil more optimistic → his CE better
Akhil

Regulatory approval 2030. India commercial launch 2031. Tier 2 (Ghana, Kenya, Brazil) 2032. Tier 3 2034. Driven by TRL-of-landscape assessment.

Ours

India launch 2033, conditional on NEDL 2031 gate. Regulatory pathway analysis: WHO PQ median ~2035 (P≤2030 ≈ 0%). NEDL listing is the binding gate, not WHO PQ timing.

Two years seems small. But under a pure-acceleration model — where the marginal DALYs are only those that happen earlier because of the AMC — a 2-year shift in launch date moves the starting point of the accumulation window and has a compounding effect through discounting.

Akhil's 2030 regulatory approval requires NeoSep-SAA to complete its culture-confirmed pivotal study, achieve WHO prequalification, and clear CDSCO registration in about four years from now. The regulatory pathway analysis we commissioned found that across 47 novel diagnostics, the median from WHO PQ to national LMIC scale is ten years, and the fastest feasible path (embedding Indian SNCU sites in the global pivotal, compressing the NEDL nomination) produces a 2031 NEDL listing — not a 2030 regulatory clearance.

Sep 2026 call — Akhil's three counterpoints: (1) A $60M facility meaningfully accelerates timelines by improving commercial return visibility, compressing firm R&D cycles. (2) Direct NEDL relationship: Akhil meets monthly with the person who writes India's NEDL, and has discussed listing NeoTest "in italics" preemptively before a product exists — a first-mover advantage that could compress the listing clock significantly. (3) Direct visibility into 12–16 Carb-X–shortlisted firms via a committee seat gives real-time pipeline intelligence unavailable to external modelers. These are substantive rather than hand-wavy rebuttals. The NEDL pre-listing conversation in particular could cut years off the regulatory gate if it materialises. Akhil offered to share the firm pipeline spreadsheet and confidential meeting notes.

That said, Akhil's optimism is not baseless. His 2031 launch year maps to India's NEDL 2031 gate, which is also our central. The difference is subtle: Akhil counts commercial sales from 2031; we count meaningful public-sector uptake from 2033 (reflecting that NEDL listing does not immediately translate to procurement). This is a matter of what "launch" means in a public-sector context.

Walking the math: $119 vs $111

The $8 gap between the two models has two offsetting forces. Our higher cost-discount rate (9% vs Georgia's 4%) makes our PV costs lower — improving our CE. Our lower adoption and c2 haircut increase CE (worsen it). These partially cancel.

Net effect
The cost-discount improvement roughly cancels the launch-year worsening. The adoption and c2 differences together account for most of the $8 residual gap. If you accept Georgia's adoption and launch assumptions but apply our 9% discount rate and c2=0.38, you get close to $100. Accept all four of our conservative choices: $111.
Payment timing structure (Sep 2026 call): The $21.2M PV cost figure is not a rounding artifact — it reflects a deliberate back-loaded payment structure. The model has approximately $21M disbursed in 2030 (the milestone prize) and ~$33M in AMC top-up payments over 2031–2035 as adoption grows. At 9% cost discount, that $33M shrinks to PV ~$19–20M. Total PV costs ≈ $34–35M nominal-but-discounted, which is what feeds the $111/DALY CE figure.

Preferred MSA/Akhil structure: full funder commitment triggered at the first milestone (~2030), held by MedAccess at 7.5–10% interest, disbursed as adoption grows through ~2045. If MedAccess earns ≥9% on held funds, the funder's opportunity cost is neutralised — the capital is not sitting idle. Alternative: $1–2M/year tranches matching adoption growth. The full-upfront structure improves CE because early commitment reduces future disbursement uncertainty; purely lump-sum upfront in 2026 would worsen CE modestly under 9% discounting.

Where we genuinely disagree

Three disagreements are matters of evidence; one is a philosophical choice; one is a shared blind spot.

Genuine empirical disagreements

Disagreement 1 — Adoption rate calibration

Akhil uses the malaria RDT as his primary adoption analogue. We use TNMSC tender histories for SNCU diagnostics. Research across comparator diagnostics in 2026 found: CBNAAT/Truenat (India's best-resourced NEDL-listed molecular test) reached only ~26% test-share after ten years. CRP/PCT in East African facilities is "barely used routinely." Brazil's mandated pulse-ox screen plateaued at ~61% SUS penetration over a decade — and that had a legal mandate and donor support.

India (HB 5.5%, Leader 15%): Research confirms these are defensible central estimates. 15% Leader is "central-to-conservative"; 5.5% HB is "contingent-central" — it holds if NEDL listing triggers procurement, but falls if NEDL listing is missed.

Brazil (35%) and SSA (20–25%): Research found no documented analogue for 20–35% NICU POCT penetration without either donor saturation or legal mandate. Revised priors from comparator evidence: Brazil ~10–18%, SSA ~8–12%. The BOTEC's Brazil/SSA adoption assumptions are optimistic by a factor of ~2×. Combined CE impact of reducing to evidence-anchored values: roughly +$15–25/DALY.

Disagreement 2 — Community adherence (c2)
This is the disagreement with the largest CE effect. Akhil takes HTA referral rates at face value; we apply an independent WHO PSBI calibration that puts real-world adherence ~25% lower. The right answer here is a field question — neither of us has data from an actual NeoTest-like deployment. The first field study (ideally in a WHO PSBI-compliant setting) will settle this.
Disagreement 3 — Launch year
Akhil models 2031 India commercial launch; we model 2033 meaningful public-sector uptake. These are measuring different things. If "launch" means first commercial sale, 2031 is plausible. If "launch" means the NEDL listing that triggers public-sector procurement at scale, 2031 is aggressive and 2033 is more defensible. For CE purposes, we think the procurement-scale definition is the right one.

Philosophical choice

Cost discount rate (2% vs 9%): This is not resolvable by evidence. Akhil's 2% is standard in academic health economics for public policy analysis. Our 9% reflects the opportunity cost of philanthropic capital. For a funder benchmarking against GiveWell or other CE programs, 9% is the right rate. For a government policymaker, 2% is more defensible. The choice shifts the CE by roughly $16/DALY.

The shared blind spot

AMR DALY credit — overestimated ~10× in both models, but immaterial for the decision

Both models credit ~10% of total benefit (0.013–0.017 DALY/test) to AMR reduction from stopping unnecessary antibiotic courses. A 2026 research audit of this component found the benefit is real but the magnitude is wrong. The mechanism — fewer unnecessary antibiotic-days → lower selection pressure → fewer future resistance-driven deaths — is valid, but the near-term DALY conversion is ~7×10⁻⁴/test (0.7%) rather than 10%, because consumption-to-resistance elasticity is 0.10 and near-term BSI-DALY causation is indirect. The correct AMR credit is ~0.001 DALY/test, not 0.013.

Headline CE impact: ~$111 → ~$113/DALY. This is an intellectual-honesty correction, not a decision-changing one.

What does NOT apply here: The framing that first-line resistance "cancels" the mortality benefit is incorrect in a de-escalation design. NeoTest is used to STOP empiric antibiotics at 48–72h, not to decide whether to start them. Empiric treatment begins at hour 0 for all danger-sign infants regardless of POCT result. The POCT tells the clinician whether to continue (positive) or stop (negative) — and a positive result can prompt escalation from amp-gent to an appropriate regimen. BARNARDS-confirmed 31% amp-gent coverage makes this escalation pathway more important, not less — and makes GARDP's NeoSep1 a genuine prerequisite for the mortality mechanism to hold.

Questions for Akhil

Seven questions that would either resolve the key disagreements or clarify whether the models are actually measuring different things.

1 Adoption calibration: why the malaria RDT rather than SNCU-specific data? biggest CE impact

Update from Sep 2026 call: Akhil clarified they used a dual analog — GeneXpert for penetration speed/time-to-market (more conservative; molecular, instrument-based, closer in complexity to this TPP) and malaria RDT for adoption slope steepness (steeper ramp; simpler commodity). SNCU was treated as a lower-bound triangulation, not the primary anchor. Their original India peak is 30% (Georgia's conservative version halved this to 15%); Brazil is 40% (Georgia halved to 20%). The model doesn't disaggregate Indian states — aggregate model with a "law of large numbers" rationale.

This is more defensible than "malaria RDT only" — GeneXpert precedent is an appropriate analog for penetration speed in a specialist clinical setting. The open question remains whether PHC/subdistrict facilities without SNCUs are realistically in the addressable population (Akhil argues yes; the TPP targets these settings too), which would make SNCU-based estimates a genuine lower bound.

2 Community adherence: does the HTA's c2 = 0.50 hold in the field? biggest CE impact

Update from Sep 2026 call: Akhil confirmed their base was WHO PSBI's 0.38, then up-titrated for two reasons: (1) an objective diagnostic reduces referral uncertainty — families/clinicians who previously doubted whether a referral was warranted get a concrete signal, improving adherence; (2) health systems are strengthening globally, easing the infrastructure constraint. The direction is UP from 0.38, not the 0.50 we assumed independently.

This is consistent with the NeoPInS evidence (clinicians do act on clear biomarker signals) and the ProACT finding (clinicians override toward MORE treatment, not less). Whether a POCT in community or PHC settings achieves the same adherence as a biomarker in a well-resourced trial SNCU remains the empirical gap. The first deployment study in a WHO PSBI–compliant setting will be decisive.

3 AMR trajectory: have you stress-tested the community mortality mechanism against Klebsiella resistance?

Update from Sep 2026 call: Akhil offered three important clarifications: (1) their model already discounts for first-line antibiotic effectiveness generally — the diagnostic's value is not solely contingent on amp-gent working; (2) first-line regimens are not static — linezolid is increasingly used in India and has a better Klebsiella resistance profile; clinicians use facility-level antibiograms to adjust empiric choice; (3) the tertiary-hospital resistance data (96–99% amp-gent resistance for Klebsiella) is skewed by NICU outbreak patients who have been in hospital for a week or more, not the general SNCU population. District-level antibiograms likely show materially lower resistance rates.

These are substantive corrections to the "shared blind spot" framing — the resistance picture is more nuanced than the binary 96–99% figure suggests. The complementarity with GARDP's NeoSep1 (antibiotic regimen research) and their existing GARDP relationship was confirmed by Akhil. An open research question: what do community-level or district-hospital antibiograms in India and Nigeria actually show, vs the tertiary-care literature?

4 Launch year 2031: what does this require on the regulatory clock?

2031 India commercial launch requires regulatory approval by 2030 — roughly four years from now. The culture-confirmed pivotal hasn't started. Median WHO PQ approval for novel diagnostics runs ~5 years from pivotal completion. Even with the fastest feasible path (Indian SNCU sites in the pivotal, expedited CDSCO review), the regulatory pathway analysis puts a 2031 NEDL listing at the optimistic end, not the central case. Can you walk us through the specific regulatory milestones that make 2031 achievable? Is 2031 first commercial sale or first public-sector procurement?

5 Post-AMC pricing: what does the model assume after the $30M cap is exhausted?

Update from Sep 2026 call: Akhil confirmed they are actively working with MedAccess on post-AMC price stability — specifically to prevent price spikes once the AMC subsidy ends. This directly addresses the structural risk flagged in our analysis. The design is not finalized yet, but MedAccess has prior experience structuring these arrangements for diagnostics (HIV RDTs, malaria RDTs, TB diagnostics) and the risk is on the radar.

The residual question is whether the MedAccess arrangement will include a binding price ceiling with a volume guarantee (the Gavi PCV AMC model — the gold standard) or a softer "best efforts" commitment. Cazabon et al. 2022 documented GeneXpert at $9.98 for ~14 years precisely because there was no binding ceiling post-AMC. The design difference matters a lot for out-year volume and the philanthropic case.

6 Success probability structure: does P(launch) = 0.65 interact with the marginal test calculation?

Your model applies P(launch) = 0.65 as an explicit discount to expected tests. Our model folds this into adoption rates rather than applying it as a separate multiplier. This is more than a presentational difference: if the counterfactual (no AMC) also has some positive probability of a product launching eventually, then the marginal DALY calculation should net out the counterfactual P(launch), not just the AMC-world P(launch). How does your counterfactual handle the case where a product launches anyway, just 7 years later — does the same 0.65 apply in both worlds?

7 AMR DALY credit: does 0.013 DALYs/test survive a resistance-reversal check? confirmed overestimate

Both models credit 0.013–0.017 DALYs/test for AMR reduction from stopping unnecessary antibiotic courses. A 2026 Fermi audit (antibiotic-days averted × consumption-to-resistance elasticity × resistance-BSI-DALY conversion) gives ~0.00073 DALYs/test — roughly 10–14× lower. The two inputs that drive this down: (a) consumption-to-AMR elasticity is ~0.10, not 1.0; (b) near-term resistance reversal from a single avoided course is near-zero because 68% of neonatal Klebsiella sepsis is horizontal-transmission-driven, not de novo selection from the marginal empiric course.

The stewardship benefit is real and belongs in a separate AMR-externality accounting line, but it should not be in the near-term DALY headline. Revised AMR credit: ~0.001 DALY/test (0.7% of total), not 0.013 (10%).

CE impact: ~$111 → ~$113/DALY. This is a calibration correction, not a model-invalidating finding. The mortality channel (79% of DALYs) is unaffected.

What "BCR = 31×" actually means — and why we prefer $/DALY

A benefit-cost ratio of 31× means: for every dollar of philanthropic cost, society gains $31 of welfare benefit. The "benefit" is DALYs valued at GDP per capita — roughly $2,100 for India, $4,400 for Kenya, $9,000 for Brazil, weighted by test volumes. Akhil's model gives $2.5 billion in discounted GDP/capita benefits (Summary tab B24) against $52.6M in PV costs (at 2% discount) — implying BCR of ~47× before probability adjustments, and ~31× after netting the P(launch) and P(commercialise) discounts.

The problem with using BCR for philanthropic allocation: it privileges interventions in poor countries (where GDP/capita is low, so DALYs valued at GDP/capita are cheap relative to cost) over rich-country interventions, which is not the right criterion. It also makes the number incomparable to GiveWell's benchmark, which uses a standard cost-per-DALY metric that doesn't depend on the GDP of the recipient country.

CE ($/DALY) solves this by asking the neutral question: how many dollars of philanthropic cost per unit of health benefit? At $111–119/DALY (marginal CE range across both models), NeoTest is just above GiveWell's top-charity bar (~$50–100) and below the syphilis program we evaluated in July ($132). That comparison is only possible with the CE metric.
